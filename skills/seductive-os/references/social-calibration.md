# Social calibration

Reading what is actually there. This is the core skill of the pack and the one with the highest ethical load: calibration is how consent becomes a capability rather than a rule ([ethics-and-consent.md](ethics-and-consent.md) section 3). Reading disinterest quickly and accurately is trained here as a skill, scored like any other, never as an obstacle to route around.

## 1. What calibration is

Calibration is the accuracy of the user's model of what is happening, held with the right amount of confidence. It has two halves and users are usually bad at a different one than they think:

- **Perception.** Noticing the signals that are actually present, including the quiet ones and the ones that contradict what the user wants.
- **Confidence discipline.** Holding the read at the strength the evidence supports, and knowing which parts of it are inference.

Set the ceiling honestly before training anything. Work on empathic accuracy consistently finds that people read each other above chance and well below reliability, with wide individual variation and modest effects **[E2]**. Nobody in this pack is going to become a reliable mind reader, and the training target is therefore not certainty but a read that is roughly as strong as the evidence behind it.

Both halves are trainable and both are measurable, which is unusual in this domain. Section 17 gives the measurement. Users who run it for six weeks reliably discover that their error is systematic and in one direction, which is the most useful single fact this pack can hand someone.

Calibration is also the factor that protects everything else. A user with high presence and low calibration produces pressure. A user with high warmth and low calibration produces confusion. It is the term in the equation that determines whether the others land as generosity or as a demand.

## 2. Observation versus inference

The discipline is mechanical and it is the entire foundation: **separate what was observed from what was concluded, out loud, every time.** The SKILL response contract requires it in every read, and it is required here because inference feels exactly like observation from the inside.

```text
OBSERVATION   something a camera would have recorded
              "they stayed for twenty minutes"
              "they asked one question about me"
              "they laughed twice"
              "they touched my arm once while making a point"

INFERENCE     everything else, including everything that feels obvious
              "they were interested"
              "they were nervous"
              "they were looking for an excuse to stay"
              "the arm touch meant something"
```

Three rules:

1. **Write the observation column first.** If it is written second, it will be assembled to support the conclusion already reached.
2. **Label every inference as an inference, in the sentence.** Not as a caveat at the end.
3. **Count, do not impression.** Numbers are the cheapest available correction to motivated reading: how many questions did each person ask, how many times did each initiate, who ended the pauses.

The camera test is the fast version: would a camera have recorded this? Warmth in a voice, no. A person turning their body toward you, yes.

## 3. A worked example, read both ways

Same interaction, two reads.

> At a friend's birthday, someone the user has just met stays in the conversation for about twenty minutes. They laugh at two things the user says. They touch the user's arm once while making a point. They ask what the user does. When the user mentions a band, they hand over their Instagram.

**Read A, the inference read.** They were into me. Twenty minutes is a long time at a party, they laughed a lot, there was physical contact, they asked about my work, and they gave me their Instagram. Clear interest, I should message tonight.

**Read B, the observation read.**

```text
OBSERVED
  Duration          ~20 min. They spent ~25 min with the previous person, same energy.
  Laughter          2 (both at jokes the whole group also laughed at)
  Contact           1 arm touch while making a point. They did the same to the host earlier.
  Questions asked   them 1 (what do you do), no follow-up to the answer
                    me 6
  Initiations       me 6, them 1 (the Instagram)
  Topic changes     me 4, them 0
  Body              stayed square to the group, not to me
  Departure         they left to get a drink and did not come back

INFERRED (labelled)
  Possible mild interest, on the Instagram alone.
  Possible ordinary party friendliness, which is the base rate here.
  The arm touch is not evidence, it is their register with everyone.

WHAT THIS LICENSES
  A low-cost legible message that makes interest answerable either way.
  It does not license reading interest, planning, or a second message if there is no reply.
```

Read A is not stupid, it is normal, and it is what almost everyone does. The difference is that Read B produces the same next action (send one clear message) with a completely different posture afterwards, and that posture is the entire difference between someone who is easy to say no to and someone who is not.

## 4. The signal categories

Count these. All of them are behavioural, none require mind reading, and none is decisive alone **[E3]**.

| Category | What to count | Why it is informative |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Proximity** | Do they close distance, hold it, or let it open | Costly and mostly unconscious |
| **Orientation** | Feet, hips and torso square to you, or square to the exit or the group | Harder to fake than a face |
| **Sustained attention** | Do they look at their phone, scan the room over your shoulder, track other conversations | Attention is the scarcest thing at a party |
| **Initiation** | Who starts the conversation, restarts it after a break, changes the topic | The best single category. See section 5 |
| **Question reciprocity** | Do they ask about you, and do they follow up on the answer | The follow-up matters more than the question |
| **Elaboration** | Are answers longer than required, or minimum viable | Elaboration is voluntary effort |
| **Effort in replies** | In messages: length relative to yours, response to content, questions returned | The clearest channel available, because effort is explicit |
| **Continuity** | Do they bring back something you said earlier, unprompted | Very high value. It means you were retained |
| **Introductions** | Do they bring you into their group, name you to friends | Social risk taken on your behalf |
| **Easing** | Do they make things easy: offering times, closing loops, removing obstacles | People make it easy for what they want |

Two structural notes. **Clusters, not items.** No single item in that table means anything; three of them moving together over a window means something. And **change over time beats level**: the same person becoming more elaborate, more initiating and more continuous across three conversations is a much stronger signal than a warm first meeting.

On brief impressions: judgements formed from very short observations do predict some outcomes better than chance, but the effects are modest, they vary a great deal by what is being judged, and several of the headline results in that literature have been revised downward **[E2]**. A quick read is a hypothesis, and this table is a list of things to count over time rather than a way to be right in ten seconds.

## 5. The asymmetry rule

The most reliable single indicator available, and the one to teach first **[E3]**:

```text
Over any window, count:
    initiations, questions asked, topics introduced, plans proposed,
    effort per message, times each person restarted after a pause

If the user is doing most of it, the user is doing most of it.
That is the finding. It does not need interpretation.
```

Roughly balanced is the shape of mutual interest. Persistently one-sided is an answer, and it is an answer even when every individual interaction was pleasant, which is exactly why users miss it: nothing bad ever happened in any single instance.

The rule has two protective properties. It is countable, so it survives motivated reasoning better than any impression. And it works identically in person and over messages, where users are most prone to reading warmth into a reply that took six hours and eleven words.

One correction: the rule is about pattern, not about a scoreboard to be enforced. Someone who is shy, busy, or unwell may initiate less for reasons that are not about the user. That is what the window is for. Over three or four exchanges the pattern is either there or it is not.

## 6. Base rates, and the most over-read signal there is

Before reading a signal, ask what fraction of people would produce it here anyway. Most of what users read as interest is behaviour with a base rate near one.

The underlying error has a name and reasonable support: people systematically under-weight the situation when explaining someone's behaviour and over-attribute it to the person **[E2]**, and the strength of that tendency varies by culture, which is itself the point. A friendly setting manufactures friendliness. Reading it as a signal about the user specifically is the default human error, applied to the one domain where the user least wants to be wrong.

| Signal | Base rate reality |
| --- | --- |
| Being friendly | Almost everyone, almost always. Carries close to zero information |
| Laughing at your joke | Standard social lubrication, particularly in groups |
| Sustained conversation at a party | Often just the absence of a better option in the room |
| Eye contact and smiling from staff | A working condition, not a signal. See below |
| Warmth from an extraverted person | Their baseline with the whole room |
| Being touched on the arm | Personal register, varies enormously by person and culture **[P]** |
| Someone being nice after alcohol | A change in them, not a change toward the user |
| Warmth in a networking or professional setting | An expected behaviour with a job attached |

**Service warmth is the single most over-read signal in the entire domain.** Someone whose job requires friendliness toward the user is not sending a signal, and their refusal can cost them their shift. [ethics-and-consent.md](ethics-and-consent.md) section 7 makes this a gate rather than a caution: the OS does not help with romantic approach where refusal is expensive. When a user reports a read on bar staff, a barista, a flight attendant, a trainer, a nurse or a support agent, name the base rate plainly and without mockery, then redirect to contexts where a no is cheap.

The correction that generalizes: **look for behaviour that is costly or selective.** Attention that is scarce elsewhere. Effort that exceeds the requirement. Something brought back from a previous conversation. Selectivity is where the information lives, because everything universal carries none.

## 7. The disinterest catalogue, and reading it fast

This is a trained skill, scored on speed and accuracy. The spine holds the catalogue of what counts as a no ([ethics-and-consent.md](ethics-and-consent.md) section 5) and it is authoritative. This section adds the earlier, softer layer, which is the layer that lets a user exit warmly before anyone has to say a word **[E3]**.

- Answers that close rather than open, consistently. Full stops where there could be doors.
- A scan of the room over the user's shoulder, more than once.
- Replies persistently shorter than the user's, with the ratio stable across days.
- Their questions stop entirely, and the user is the only one asking.
- The pivot to "we": their friends, their group, their plans, none of which include the user.
- The phone checked mid-answer.
- A polite laugh with a still face, particularly repeated.
- Nothing from a previous conversation ever comes back.
- Plans that are always "sometime" and never a time, twice.
- Enthusiasm about the topic that does not extend to the person.
- The step back that is not re-closed. The bag picked up and held.

**The speed rule.** Act on the first cluster, not the fifth. Two or three of these in a short window is enough. The asymmetry of costs is the argument, and it is worth stating to the user directly:

```text
Reading disinterest EARLY and being wrong   -> you were warm, you left, they may follow up.
                                               Recoverable. Cost: small.
Reading disinterest LATE and being wrong    -> they had to escalate to being explicit,
                                               possibly more than once. They now had to
                                               manage you. Cost: theirs, and not recoverable.
```

The user who exits early and cleanly is also the user who gets more direct signals in future, from everyone, because people are far more direct with someone who has demonstrated a no is cheap. This is the compounding argument for reading fast, and it is true **[E3]**.

Score it in the debrief as a process metric: at what point in the interaction was the signal first available, and at what point did the user act on it. The gap is the trainable quantity.

## 8. Context before person

Read the setting before reading anyone in it. Most calibration failures are context failures wearing a person-shaped costume.

Three questions, in order:

1. **Is romantic approach appropriate in this setting at all?** Some contexts are simply not that, and the answer does not depend on the individual. A funeral, a support group, a professional conference session, a class the user teaches, a hospital, a place of worship for many people, anywhere the other person is working.
2. **Who else is present, and what does that cost?** A partner, colleagues, family, their boss, a group they will still be in tomorrow. A no delivered in front of an audience is expensive, which makes any ask in front of one a pressure move ([ethics-and-consent.md](ethics-and-consent.md) section 9).
3. **What did they come here to do?** Train, work, study, commute, grieve, dance with their friends, get through a shift. A person mid-task is not available, and interrupting a task to be evaluated is a cost imposed without asking.

| Context | Cheap refusal | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Party, friends of friends | Yes | The best available context. Shared social proof, easy exits |
| Recurring hobby, class, league | Yes | Repeated contact, and section 4 of [attraction-fundamentals.md](attraction-fundamentals.md) says why this is the strongest structure |
| Bar or club, mutual approach | Usually | Intoxication caveats apply ([ethics-and-consent.md](ethics-and-consent.md) section 8) |
| Street, queue, public transport | Mixed | Depends entirely on whether they can leave and are not mid-task |
| Gym mid-set, library, headphones on | No | Mid-task. Do not |
| Workplace, no reporting line | Constrained | One ask, low-stakes setting, dropped completely on anything short of yes |
| Workplace with a power line, or any duty of care | No | Gate 1 fails. The OS declines |
| Anyone at work serving the user | No | Section 6, and the spine's section 7 |

## 9. Groups and the friend read

The friend group is the highest-bandwidth signal source available and users mostly ignore it or treat it as an obstacle. It is neither.

- **They are participants, not gatekeepers.** Treating a group as a defence to be bypassed is the frame that produces the isolation tactics refused in [refusals.md](refusals.md) tier 1. Never separate a person from their group.
- **The group's response is fast and accurate information.** If friends make space, angle to include the user, ask them questions, or leave the two of them talking, the person has communicated something to their friends. If friends close ranks, reappear repeatedly, or extract the person with a task, they have communicated something else and they are usually acting on a request.
- **The rescue is a no.** A friend arriving with a reason to leave is a pre-arranged exit in most social groups. Treat it as final, be warm, let them go, and do not test it.
- **Being introduced is a strong signal.** Bringing the user into their circle costs the person something socially, which is what makes it informative.
- **Talk to the group.** It is more enjoyable, it is lower pressure on the person, and it produces vastly better information than a cornered one-to-one. Section 16 of [conversation-craft.md](conversation-craft.md) has the mechanics.

## 10. Variance: culture, neurotype, personality

**[P]**, and large. Signal expression varies so much between people that a fixed decoding table is actively misleading.

| Source of variance | What changes |
| --- | --- |
| Culture | Eye contact norms, touch norms, personal space, directness of refusal, whether enthusiasm is expressed or restrained, how much politeness is obligatory |
| Autistic communication | Flat affect is not disinterest. Reduced eye contact is not avoidance. An extended monologue about a specific interest is frequently high engagement, not obliviousness. Facial expression may not track internal state |
| ADHD | Interruption is often enthusiasm rather than dominance. Attention drift is not always a verdict on the conversation |
| Anxiety and shyness | Under-expression of real interest. Short answers from fear, not disinterest. Initiation suppressed even when interest is high |
| Introversion and extraversion | Extraverts run warm with everyone, which inflates the base rate. Introverts run cool with everyone, which deflates it |
| Masking | Someone masking is producing signals that do not track their state, in both directions, at a cost to themselves |
| The user's own neurotype | An autistic user reading allistic signals is doing translation work, and a table of "signs they like you" is a bad translator |

The rule this produces, and it is the most important operational consequence in the document:

```text
The higher the variance, the earlier you ASK instead of decode.
Ambiguity is not resolved by decoding harder. It is resolved by making
something answerable, in words, at low cost.
```

That applies both ways. A user whose own signals are hard to read should say the thing rather than send it, and that is a strength strategy rather than a workaround. It also protects them: unread interest that was never stated is the mechanism behind years of hovering ([ethics-and-consent.md](ethics-and-consent.md) section 9).

## 11. Interest that is real and not romantic

Extremely common, routinely misfiled, and worth naming as its own category rather than as a failure.

People can genuinely enjoy the user, seek them out, remember things about them and want them around, with no romantic interest at all. That is a real relationship and a real compliment, and the signals for it overlap heavily with romantic interest in the early categories of section 4. The distinguishing evidence usually arrives only after interest is made legible, which is another argument for section 16.

How to accept it:

- **Do not withdraw warmth punitively.** The abrupt cooling after a no is visible, it is unkind, and it tells the person exactly what the friendship was contingent on. It is also the engine of the resentment pattern the spine names in commitment 2.
- **Do not keep hovering.** Waiting near someone in the hope that the answer changes is not patience, it is an unstated demand, and both people can feel it.
- **Decide honestly.** Do they want this friendship for itself? Yes is a good answer and no is an acceptable one. Continuing while quietly waiting is the only bad answer.
- **The self-respect version:** stepping back from a friendship the user cannot hold cleanly is legitimate, provided it is done without punishment and without an explanation designed to produce guilt.

See [rejection-resilience.md](rejection-resilience.md) for the recovery half of this.

## 12. The two calibration errors

They are not symmetrical, they have different causes, and they need opposite corrections. Diagnose which one the user has before giving any calibration advice, because the standard advice for one makes the other worse.

| | Over-reading | Under-reading |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The error** | Seeing interest that is not there | Missing interest that is there |
| **Produces** | Pressure, unwanted persistence, discomfort for the other person | Missed connection, chronic ambiguity, years of hovering |
| **Cost falls on** | The other person. This is what makes it the dangerous one | The user, mostly, plus a person who was never given a choice |
| **Typical user** | Confident, motivated, low exposure to clear refusals | Anxious, self-critical, high exposure to imagined refusals |
| **Feels like** | Certainty | Realism |
| **The tell** | Every read comes back positive. Contradicting evidence gets explained | Every read comes back negative. Positive evidence gets explained |
| **Correction** | Section 13 | Section 14 |

Note the shared structure: in both errors the user's read is not tracking evidence, it is tracking a prior. The drill in section 17 exposes which prior, with numbers, which is why it is the highest-value practice in this file.

Expect resistance from the over-reader in particular, because confidence in social judgement is generally poorly related to its accuracy: people are routinely more certain than their hit rate justifies, and the feeling of certainty is not a usable signal of being right **[E2]**. Say that plainly rather than arguing with a specific read. The argument you cannot win is about one interaction; the argument you can win is about a scoreboard the user keeps themselves.

## 13. Over-reading, the dangerous one

Corrections, in order of force:

1. **The written split is mandatory,** not optional, for a user with this error. Observation column first, then inference, per section 2. Writing it is the intervention; doing it in the head does not work.
2. **Count initiations.** Section 5. This is the fastest reality check available and it is very hard to argue with.
3. **The stranger test.** Would someone watching from the next table describe this as mutual interest? If the user has to explain the context for it to sound like interest, it is inference.
4. **Never act on a single signal.** A laugh, a touch, a look, a compliment. Clusters over a window, or nothing.
5. **Assume the base rate.** Section 6. Start from "this is how they are with everyone" and require evidence to move off it.
6. **Ask instead of accumulating.** The over-reader's instinct is to gather more evidence, which means more time spent near someone who has not been given a choice. Make interest legible, once, and take the answer. Section 16.
7. **The disinterest catalogue is the priority read.** Train section 7 before anything about detecting interest. A user whose disinterest detection is fast is safe to have high confidence elsewhere.

Then the harder conversation, if the pattern is severe: repeatedly reading interest that is not there, continuing after signals, or needing to be told no explicitly more than once are not calibration errors any more. Route to [ethics-and-consent.md](ethics-and-consent.md) and, if it includes monitoring, showing up where someone will be, or contact after a request to stop, stop coaching and route to [safety-and-boundaries.md](safety-and-boundaries.md), labelled **[C]**.

## 14. Under-reading, the common one

The most frequent profile in this pack, and it usually arrives disguised as humility or realism.

Corrections:

1. **Name the bias, with their own data.** Run section 17 for four to six weeks. An under-reader's numbers are unmistakable, and being shown their own systematic underestimate does more than any reassurance.
2. **Raise the prior deliberately.** If the user's default read is "they are just being nice", the corrected default is "I do not know", which is both more accurate and more actionable.
3. **The 40 percent rule.** If the user is 40 percent confident there is interest, that is more than enough to make interest legible. Certainty is not the threshold, and waiting for it is the mechanism of the whole problem.
4. **Count what they discount.** Under-readers systematically discard the strongest categories: continuity, easing, and introductions (section 4). Have them look specifically for those three, because those are the ones their prior deletes.
5. **Reframe the cost.** They believe the cost of a wrong positive is humiliation. The actual cost is one clear sentence and a warm no, which is survivable and is trained in [rejection-resilience.md](rejection-resilience.md). The cost of the current strategy is every missed connection plus the accumulated resentment of never having asked.
6. **Ambiguity is not neutral.** Chronic ambiguity is a choice with a cost to both people, and the spine says so explicitly ([ethics-and-consent.md](ethics-and-consent.md) section 9). Hovering is not respectful, it is avoidant.

If the under-reading is driven by fear rather than by a bad model, the real work is in [anxiety-and-avoidance.md](anxiety-and-avoidance.md) and [inner-game-and-self-worth.md](inner-game-and-self-worth.md). Calibration drills will not fix an accuracy problem whose cause is that acting on an accurate read is terrifying.

## 15. Checking a read out loud

Checking a read is a normal conversational move, and users believe it is heavy because they imagine the heaviest possible version of it. The light version has four properties, and they are the same four the spine requires of any invitation:

```text
SPECIFIC    about one concrete thing, not about the state of everything
OWNED       your read and your interest, stated as yours
LIGHT       one sentence, said at the same volume and pace as the rest of the conversation
ANSWERABLE  a no can be given in three words, and you have visibly stopped talking
```

Worked example, illustrative only:

> "I have enjoyed this a lot and I would like to take you for a coffee sometime this week. No pressure either way."

Then stop. The stopping is the technique. What ruins the check is what comes after it: the qualification, the joke that dilutes it, the second version, the explanation of why it is fine if they say no. All of those transfer management work to the other person and make the no more expensive, which is the opposite of the intent.

Hand the user the four properties and let them write their own sentence. Never hand them the sentence ([refusals.md](refusals.md) section 3). Run [consent_check.md](../protocols/consent_check.md) before any check in a context where refusal might not be cheap.

Also legitimate, and lighter: checking a read about the interaction rather than about a plan. "Am I keeping you from your friends?" gives a real exit and gets a real answer. Use it when the read is genuinely unclear and the stakes are still low.

## 16. Legibility beats decoding

The governing principle of the whole document, and the one to state last so it lands.

**When in doubt, make interest legible and let them answer. Do not decode harder.**

Decoding harder has four failure modes and legibility has none of them:

| Decoding harder | Making it legible |
| --- | --- |
| Requires more time near someone who has not been given a choice | Ends the ambiguity in one sentence |
| Accuracy is capped by variance (section 10) and by the user's own prior | Gets the answer from the only reliable source |
| Produces the hovering pattern and its resentment | Produces an answer, either of which is usable |
| The user carries the uncertainty and the other person carries the pressure | The other person gets a genuine choice, cheaply |

There is also a positive argument, not only a defensive one. Reciprocity of liking is among the better-replicated findings in this literature: learning that someone likes you tends to increase liking of them **[E1]**, provided it reads as selective rather than indiscriminate **[E2]**. Legible, specific interest is therefore not a concession made because decoding failed. It is the better-supported move ([attraction-fundamentals.md](attraction-fundamentals.md) section 4).

The failure mode to guard against is the user who takes this as licence to ask repeatedly. Legibility means **one** clear, low-cost, answer-tolerant statement, and then the answer stands ([ethics-and-consent.md](ethics-and-consent.md), commitment 4). Legibility is not a substitute for calibration either: calibration decides whether the context permits an ask at all (section 8), and legibility is what happens inside a context that does.

## 17. The accuracy drill

The highest-value practice in this file. It converts calibration from a feeling into a measured skill, and it is the only way a user discovers the direction of their own error.

The method is borrowed rather than invented: in forecasting research, writing explicit probability estimates and scoring them against outcomes improves calibration, and the improvement comes specifically from the feedback loop rather than from the act of predicting **[E2]**. Whether that transfers to reading romantic interest has not been tested and the OS does not claim it has **[E3]**. What it does reliably produce, observed in practice, is a user who can see the direction of their own bias instead of arguing about it.

```text
BEFORE or immediately after an interaction, write one prediction with a number:
    "They would say yes to coffee: 30%"
    "They were being friendly, not interested: 70%"
    "They will reply within a day: 60%"

Write the OBSERVED column beside it (section 2). Two lines is enough.

LOG the outcome when you learn it. Some never resolve. Mark those unresolved
and leave them; a missing outcome is not evidence in either direction.

SCORE every four weeks, in buckets:
    Of the predictions you called at 70%, how many were right?
    ~70% right      -> well calibrated
    Much lower      -> overconfident in that direction
    Much higher     -> underconfident in that direction

READ the pattern:
    Overconfident on interest        -> over-reading. Section 13.
    Underconfident on interest       -> under-reading. Section 14.
    Well calibrated, still no dates  -> the problem is VOLUME or LEGIBILITY,
                                        not reading. Stop working on calibration.
```

That last branch is why the drill is worth the effort. It is the only instrument in the pack that can tell a user their reading is fine and their real problem is elsewhere, which saves months of work on the wrong factor ([attraction-fundamentals.md](attraction-fundamentals.md) section 16).

Practical notes: eight to twelve predictions over four weeks is enough to see a direction, and precision is not the point. Log through [interaction_debrief.md](../protocols/interaction_debrief.md), keep it in the same place, and keep third parties to a first name with no identifying detail, per the SKILL privacy rule.

## 18. Coaching notes

- **Calibration is not a defensive skill.** Coach it as the thing that makes the user good to be around, not as a compliance layer. A user who reads well is relaxing to talk to, because the other person is not managing them.
- **Never present a decoding table as truth.** Section 4 is a list of things to count, not a lookup table of meanings. If a user starts quoting signals as rules, that is the failure mode, and the correction is section 10.
- **Do not let calibration become self-monitoring.** A user running a signal checklist mid-conversation has moved their attention inward and destroyed the presence the reading depends on ([presence-and-embodiment.md](presence-and-embodiment.md) section 18). Read in the debrief, be present in the room. One instruction at a time, and it points outward.
- **Score the read, not the outcome.** Per the SKILL scorecard, a correctly read no acted on quickly is a successful rep and should be logged as one.
- **Both errors deserve the same tone.** The over-reader is usually not predatory, they are hopeful and untrained. The under-reader is usually not humble, they are frightened. Neither is helped by moralising, and both are helped by their own numbers.
- **Escalate what needs escalating.** Monitoring another person, contact after a request for no contact, or repeated approaches after clear refusals are not calibration problems. Stop, name it, and route via [safety-and-boundaries.md](safety-and-boundaries.md), labelled **[C]**.
