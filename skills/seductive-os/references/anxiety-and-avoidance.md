# Anxiety and avoidance

The document for the user who knows exactly what to do and does not do it. Everything else in this pack assumes contact with real people, so this reference sits upstream of most of it: a user with zero exposure does not have a conversation problem, they have an avoidance problem wearing a conversation problem's clothes.

Written on top of [ethics-and-consent.md](ethics-and-consent.md). Section 15 carries the constraint specific to this document, and it is not negotiable: exposure practice never uses another person as equipment.

## 1. The avoidance loop

Draw it once, because most users have never seen it laid out and recognizing it is half the work.

```text
CUE         Someone attractive, a party, a match, a chance to speak.
PREDICTION  "This will go badly and it will mean something about me."
ANXIETY     Heart, chest, heat, tunnel attention, urge to leave.
AVOIDANCE   Do not go / do not speak / leave early / text instead /
            wait for a better moment / research more first.
RELIEF      Immediate, strong, and the whole problem.
LEARNING    "Avoiding worked." The prediction is never tested.
NEXT TIME   The cue arrives earlier, the anxiety arrives faster, and the
            threshold for what counts as too hard drops.
```

The critical property: **the loop worsens without any bad experience occurring.** A user can spend three years becoming steadily more anxious while never once being turned down, because the reinforcement comes from the escape, not from any outcome in the world. This is why "nothing bad has ever actually happened to me" is not reassuring evidence and must not be offered as such. It is a symptom of the loop running successfully.

## 2. Relief is the reinforcer

Say it plainly, because it reframes the user's whole history in one sentence: **the relief that follows avoidance is a reward, and it is training them.** Negative reinforcement, a behaviour strengthened by the removal of something unpleasant, is among the most solidly established mechanisms in behavioural science **[E1]**, and it is the engine of the loop. Every decision not to go delivers a large, immediate, well-timed reward for not going, and an intention formed the next morning is no match for it.

Three consequences:

1. **Motivation is not the missing ingredient.** The user is being trained several times a week in the opposite direction, and adding willpower does not change a reinforcement schedule.
2. **The decision point is earlier than they think.** Standing outside the venue, the outcome is largely determined. The tractable decision was made when the plan was set, which is why practice is scheduled and committed in advance ([weekly_practice.md](../protocols/weekly_practice.md)).
3. **Partial avoidance still reinforces.** Going but staying with the friend who talks, going but leaving in ten minutes, going but only texting: each delivers the relief and blocks the learning (section 5).

## 3. Graded exposure: the mechanism that works

The counter is repeated, planned, non-escaped contact with the feared situation, at a difficulty the user can actually stay in. Exposure-based practice is one of the best supported interventions in the anxiety literature **[E1]**, and the pack teaches the coaching-level version, deliberately far below the clinical one.

Two models, with the honest state of each. **Habituation:** anxiety declines within a rep if the user stays in it, and declines across reps; classic and well replicated as a description **[E1]**. **Inhibitory learning and expectancy violation:** the active ingredient is learning that the predicted catastrophe did not occur, so the design goal is maximum surprise rather than minimum discomfort; promising, increasingly favoured, not settled **[E2]**.

Hold both: stay in the rep long enough for the anxiety to come down, and write the prediction down first so the disconfirmation is legible (section 6). Both are cheap, and doing both beats arguing about which is right.

**The boundary, stated up front.** This is coaching-grade exposure for ordinary approach anxiety. Clinical-level anxiety needs a clinician, and clinician-led exposure is a different, more careful, more structured thing. The OS does not run it, does not simulate it, and routes instead.

## 4. Ordinary approach anxiety versus a clinical condition

Do not diagnose. Do use the functional markers to decide whether to coach or route, because coaching someone through a disorder is a failure of care dressed as encouragement.

| Marker | Ordinary approach anxiety | Route out **[C]** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Domains | Romantic contexts, sometimes parties | Work, phone calls, shops, classrooms, eating in public |
| Behaviour | Nervous, still goes sometimes | Systematic sustained avoidance of most social situations |
| Physical | Heart, heat, dry mouth, settles | Panic attacks, trembling, nausea, symptoms feared in themselves |
| Impairment | Fewer dates than wanted | Job, study, friendships or daily function measurably damaged |
| Duration | Situational, variable | Persistent over six months or more, stable across contexts |
| Self-view | "I get nervous" | Persistent conviction of being scrutinized and judged |
| Afterwards | Some replaying | Hours or days of detailed self-critical review |
| Coping | Nerves | Alcohol or another substance needed before contact |
| Mood | Frustrated | Hopeless, depressed, unable to see a way through |

Any right-hand cell present, in any row: **stop the coaching path and route.** Keep the wording short, plain and non-clinical.

> What you are describing goes past what a coaching pack should handle. It is common, it is one of the more treatable things there is, and the right tool is a professional who does this properly. I am not going to run an exposure ladder alongside that, because doing it badly can make it worse.

Then route to [safety-and-boundaries.md](safety-and-boundaries.md) and stop. Do not name a disorder as though assessing for it, do not continue a practice plan in parallel, and do not soften the route into a suggestion the user can decline while the plan runs anyway. Independent immediate stop conditions: panic attacks, self-harm or thoughts of not being here, an eating disorder or body dysmorphic pattern, obsessive fixation on a specific person, any surveillance behaviour, and alcohol or another substance as a precondition for social contact.

## 5. Safety behaviours

Anything the user does to make the feared situation survivable that also prevents them discovering they would have survived it anyway. They feel like coping. They are the mechanism by which a fear survives hundreds of exposures intact **[E1]**.

| The safety behaviour | What it stops them learning |
| --- | --- |
| Two drinks before speaking to anyone | That they can do it sober, the only version that generalizes |
| The phone checked in every pause | That a pause is survivable and nobody was looking |
| The friend who does the talking | That their own opening works, since it was never tested |
| The rehearsed line | That they can respond to a real person in real time |
| Only going out in a group | That they are approachable alone, which is when people approach |
| Only ever texting | That the in-person version is manageable |
| Standing by the exit or the bar all night | That being in the room is fine |
| Hours of preparation beforehand | That conversation makes itself out of what they hear |
| Leaving after exactly ten minutes | That anxiety comes down if they stay |
| No eye contact while speaking | That eye contact is met, not punished |

Two honest nuances, because a flat prohibition is wrong. Some literature supports a **judicious** safety behaviour early to make a rung possible at all, then removing it deliberately **[E2]**, so the pack's rule is: it may be used to get onto a rung, never to stay on one, and its removal is written into the plan with a date. Alcohol is the exception with no judicious version: it blunts the learning, it is the single most common way this is maintained for a decade, and if the user cannot socialize without it that is a **[C]** and it routes.

The diagnostic for any rep: **what did I do so that I would be alright, and what did it stop me finding out?**

## 6. Writing the prediction down first

Cheap, unglamorous, and the highest-yield instruction here. Ten seconds in a phone note, before the rep.

```text
PREDICTION SHEET
What I think will happen: ______   How bad, 0-10: __   How likely, 0-100%: __
AFTER
What actually happened: ______    How bad, 0-10: __
Anxiety at start / peak / end: __ / __ / __
What I now know that I did not know before: ______
```

Without the written prediction, the mind rewrites history to protect the fear: "it went fine, but that one was easy", "they were just being polite", "it would have gone badly with someone I actually liked". A prediction on paper cannot be edited afterwards, and the gap between the predicted 8 and the actual 2 is the learning **[E1/E2]**.

Two patterns to look for across a dozen sheets. **Predicted badness sits far above actual**, which is the point of the exercise and far more persuasive shown as the user's own data than offered as reassurance. **The feared catastrophe has never actually occurred and they have not noticed**, so ask directly how many times in all their reps the thing they feared happened. The answer is usually zero, and it usually surprises them.

Record what the catastrophe actually is, precisely. "It would be bad" is not testable. "They would visibly recoil, their friends would laugh, and I would have to walk past them again" is, and once written it can be checked against reality.

## 7. The ten-rung ladder

Start far below "approach a stranger you are attracted to". That is roughly rung nine, and every user who begins there confirms their fear and quits. Every rung below is an ordinary interaction that is completely fine for the other person regardless of how it goes, which is a design constraint and not a coincidence (section 15).

```text
1  EYE CONTACT AND A NOD. Ten people, any context, no words.
2  GREETINGS. Hello or good morning to five people passing close by:
   a neighbour, a dog walker, someone in a lift.
3  ASK STAFF A QUESTION, three times. Where is the milk, what time do you
   close. People whose job includes answering, so an answer is guaranteed.
4  ONE SENTENCE TO A PERSON IN A QUEUE OR A WAITING ROOM. A remark about
   the shared situation. No follow-up required.
5  A COMMENT AT A SHARED ACTIVITY. Class, gym, climbing wall, choir,
   board game night, dog park. About the thing you are both doing.
6  A THIRTY-SECOND EXCHANGE. Comment, their reply, one follow-up question,
   then leave first, while it is still going well.
7  A REAL CONVERSATION WITH NO ROMANTIC COMPONENT. Five minutes or more,
   with anyone. The biggest rung in the ladder. Worth ten reps.
8  JOIN AN EXISTING GROUP CONVERSATION. Stand at the edge, listen,
   contribute one thing.
9  A CONVERSATION WITH SOMEONE YOU FIND ATTRACTIVE, WITH NO ASK.
   No number, no invitation. Learn that attraction being present does
   not change your ability.
10 MAKE INTEREST LEGIBLE. One clear low-pressure invitation
   ([ethics-and-consent.md](ethics-and-consent.md) section 9), warmly delivered,
   clean exit ready either way ([rejection-resilience.md](rejection-resilience.md) section 6).
```

Rungs 1 to 4 are not warm-ups for the impatient to skip. They are where the user learns that ordinary social contact does not produce the predicted catastrophe, and every later rung rests on that. Adjust to the real starting point: a user fine at work and frozen only romantically finds 1 to 5 boring and starts at 6, while a user who cannot ask a shop assistant a question is at the right rung and also gives a signal to re-read section 4.

## 8. Rules for running the ladder

```text
1  REPEAT UNTIL IT DROPS. Stay on a rung until anxiety comes down WITHIN
   the rep and the rung is boring. Usually five to ten reps, often more.
   Boredom is the promotion criterion, not the absence of nerves.
2  NEVER SKIP. A skipped rung is paid for later, as confirmation of the fear.
3  NO SAFETY BEHAVIOURS on the rung you are actually working. Sober, phone
   away, alone, unrehearsed.
4  SUCCESS IS DOING IT, not it going well. A rep where the user spoke and
   the other person was uninterested is a COMPLETED rep, scored as one.
5  PREDICT FIRST. Ten seconds. Every rep.
6  STAY PAST THE URGE TO LEAVE, by ten seconds. Leaving at peak anxiety is
   the escape that reinforces the loop.
7  FREQUENCY BEATS INTENSITY. Three small reps in a week beats one heroic
   evening followed by three weeks off.
8  LEAVE ON A GOOD ONE where you can, but never leave to escape.
9  LOG IT the same day. Evidence is the only thing that moves a belief.
10 STOP RULE. If distress rises across weeks rather than falling, the ladder
   stops and section 4 is re-read.
```

Set a weekly rep target in writing, at a number the user is confident of hitting. A missed target teaches the same lesson the loop already teaches, so undershoot on purpose.

## 9. Three different problems: before, during, after

Users call all three "my anxiety". They have different shapes and different tools, and using the wrong tool is why nothing has worked.

| | Anticipatory | In the moment | Post-mortem |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| When | Hours or days before | Seconds to minutes | Hours to days after |
| Content | Prediction and dread | Arousal, blank mind | Replay and self-criticism |
| Peak | Just before the event, then drops | Early, then falls if you stay | At night, worst alone |
| Wrong tool | Analysis, reassurance, more preparation | Trying to calm down | Working out what went wrong |
| Right tool | Shrink the decision, commit in advance | Body and outward attention | Time-box, one action, contact |
| Section | 10 | 11 | 12 |

The most useful single fact: **anticipatory anxiety usually peaks just before the event and drops once it starts.** Tell the user in advance so they can notice it happening, because the drop is the disconfirmation and it is invisible if nobody flagged it.

## 10. Anticipatory anxiety

The dread before is not information about how the event will go, it is the cost of having an imagination.

- **Shrink the decision.** They are not deciding whether to transform their social life, they are deciding whether to leave the house at 19:40. Get it down to a physical action with a time attached.
- **Decide once, in advance, in writing.** The commitment is made on a calm day and the anxious hour gets no vote. Pair with a friend who expects them, or a class already paid for.
- **Cap the preparation** at ten minutes, on logistics only. Over-preparing is a safety behaviour (section 5).
- **Set the exit condition in advance:** "forty minutes and two people, then I may leave." A defined end makes entry cheap, and it must be time-based, because a feeling-based exit is an escape.
- **Do not seek reassurance.** Asking a friend whether it will go fine is the extraction described in [inner-game-and-self-worth.md](inner-game-and-self-worth.md) section 5. It works for ninety seconds and trains dependence.
- **Move.** A walk before, not a sit and a think.

## 11. In-the-moment anxiety and the five-minute reset

The goal is not calm. The goal is enough capacity to act while anxious, because waiting for calm is another form of waiting to be ready. Run [reset_5_min.md](../protocols/reset_5_min.md).

```text
THE RESET
1 FEET     Weight into both feet. Notice the floor. Two seconds.
2 EXHALE   Out longer than in. Four in, six or eight out, six rounds.
           The long exhale is the part that does the work.
3 UNCLENCH Jaw, shoulders, hands. Drop them once, deliberately.
4 NAME     "This is adrenaline. It is uncomfortable and it is not danger."
5 OUTWARD  Three specific things in the room that are actually there.
6 ONE ACT  The smallest next physical action. Walk to the bar. Say hello.
           Do not decide the evening. Decide the next ten seconds.
```

Notes for the coach. Do not teach breath holds, hyperventilation drills or anything requiring supervision: the long exhale is sufficient and safe. Do not tell the user to hide the anxiety, because concealment consumes the attention the interaction needs, mild nerves are unremarkable to almost everyone, and users overestimate how visible their anxiety is, consistently and by a large margin **[E1]**. The baseline version of the body work belongs to [presence-and-embodiment.md](presence-and-embodiment.md); a user who only runs the reset in emergencies has not built it.

## 12. The post-mortem loop

The hours afterwards, replaying and cringing. In the social anxiety literature this is a well documented maintaining factor rather than a harmless habit: the review is biased toward the user's own perceived failures and it becomes the memory of the event **[E1]**. Its signature is that it feels like learning and produces no action.

```text
CLOSING THE LOOP
1 ONE DEBRIEF, TIME-BOXED. Fifteen minutes, written, once, using
  [interaction_debrief.md](../protocols/interaction_debrief.md). When the timer ends, it ends.
2 LESSON OR NONE. Most reps yield NONE. That is a valid, common result.
3 THE PREDICTION SHEET. Predicted against actual (section 6). This is the
  antidote, because it is the one record the loop cannot rewrite.
4 TEST THE MEMORY. "What would a bored bystander have seen?"
  Almost always: two people talking, then not talking.
5 ONE ACTION. Book the next rep now, not tomorrow.
6 MOVE AND CONTACT. Activity, then say it out loud to one person.
7 NO CHECKING. Their profile, the thread, the group photos.
```

If the loop will not close, runs for days, or has fixated on one specific person, that is section 4 territory rather than a technique problem.

## 13. The freeze

Distinct from anxiety and needing its own answer, because the usual advice does not touch it. The user is standing there, the moment is available, and the body will not move, frequently followed by self-contempt that does more damage than the freeze.

1. **Do not fight it in the moment.** Argument loses. Shift to something physical and small: move where you are standing, get a drink, go outside, walk one lap. Movement restores access to action more reliably than reasoning.
2. **Take a micro-rep instead.** Not the approach. Say something to anyone: the bartender, the person next to you, the coat check. One rung lower, immediately, so the evening is not filed as a failure.
3. **Lower the threshold in advance.** Freezing usually means the rung was too high. Rebuild around rung minus two.
4. **Prepare a physical entry action, not a sentence.** "Walk to the end of the bar and stand there" is executable while frozen. "Say something interesting" is not.
5. **Name the pattern without contempt.** The freeze is common and it is not cowardice; the self-attack afterwards is what does lasting damage. Treat it with truth, humanity and responsibility ([inner-game-and-self-worth.md](inner-game-and-self-worth.md) section 11).
6. **Recurrent total freezing across many attempts** is a marker to re-read section 4, particularly alongside physical symptoms.

## 14. Perfectionism and the deferral

The most respectable form of avoidance and the hardest to catch, because it is made of things that are genuinely good: "when I am in better shape", "when I have a better job", "after a few more of these", "when my flat is somewhere I could bring someone", "once I have sorted my teeth", "when I am less busy", "when I feel more confident" ([inner-game-and-self-worth.md](inner-game-and-self-worth.md) section 14).

Each is real, some are worth doing, none are entry conditions. Three things to say. **The deferral is doing work:** it supplies relief today plus a virtuous frame, which is why it is stickier than plain avoidance. **The goalposts move:** ask directly what happened last time they hit one of these conditions, and the answer is nearly always that a new prerequisite appeared. **The two tracks run in parallel:** get fit and do rung 3 this week, because the fitness is a genuine good ([style-and-self-presentation.md](style-and-self-presentation.md), section 16) and not a prerequisite, and treating it as one is how three years pass.

The related trap: refusing to do a rep badly. Early reps are supposed to be clumsy, and a user who will only do it once they can do it well has installed a condition guaranteeing they never start.

## 15. The ethics of exposure practice

The constraint specific to this document, binding every ladder the OS produces. **Other people are not equipment**, and the user's training is not a reason for anyone else to have a worse minute.

1. **Every rung is an interaction that is fine for the other person regardless of outcome.** That is why the ladder is built from nods, greetings, questions with answers, and remarks about a shared situation. None of them impose.
2. **The ask must be real.** Asking a shop assistant something the user already knows is a small deception performed on someone at work. Ask something real, or pick another rung.
3. **Nobody is a prop.** No filming, no friends watching and scoring, no telling someone afterwards that they were an exercise, no stunts designed to be refused ([rejection-resilience.md](rejection-resilience.md) section 13).
4. **No practising on people who cannot leave.** Staff on shift beyond a brief question, anyone captive, anyone under a power asymmetry: excluded exactly as in [ethics-and-consent.md](ethics-and-consent.md) section 7. Service warmth is a working condition, not a signal, and not a training ground.
5. **A no ends the rep.** Exposure value is never increased by extending past a signal, and reading the signal is itself the skill ([social-calibration.md](social-calibration.md)).
6. **Rungs 9 and 10 run under the full consent gate**, including the check that the user is reading rather than inventing.
7. **The floor holds:** neutral or better for having met the user, on every rung, including the ones done badly.

Correct any plan that violates these and hand back the version that works. The intent is nearly always benign, and the corrected rung is usually a better exposure anyway, because a real interaction produces a real result.

## 16. The physical substrate

Not a side note. The substrate sets the floor for how much anxiety the user carries before anything social happens.

| Factor | What is established | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sleep | Short sleep increases next-day emotional reactivity and anxiety **[E1]** | Fix sleep before diagnosing a social problem. A user on five hours will not win this |
| Caffeine | Reliably raises arousal, can provoke anxiety symptoms and panic-like states in sensitive people at higher doses **[E1]** | Cut it on rep days or move it earlier. Individual sensitivity varies enormously **[P]** |
| Alcohol | Works acutely, maintains the fear long term, the classic safety behaviour **[E2]** | Reps are sober. If they cannot be, that is **[C]** and it routes |
| Exercise | Regular activity is associated with lower anxiety, with reasonable trial support **[E1]** | Anything they will keep doing. It also generates rungs 5 and 7 for free |
| Food and water | Underrated. Low blood sugar and dehydration are routinely mistaken for anxiety | Eat before, not after |

The protocol layer belongs to Health & Energy OS, and the handoff is explicit: if sleep, alcohol or training is the actual bottleneck, the coaching goes there first and the ladder waits. A magnetism practice built on four hours of sleep is built on sand.

## 17. When the ladder stalls

A stall is information. Read it before adding effort.

| The stall | What it usually means | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cannot start at all, for weeks | Bottom rung too high, or this is section 4 | Halve the rung. If still impossible, re-read section 4 |
| Reps happen, anxiety does not drop | Safety behaviours in place, or reps too far apart | Audit section 5, raise frequency |
| Fine on 1 to 6, wall at 7 | The romantic component, not the social one | Stay on 7 with attractive people present, no ask. Volume |
| Fine in practice, freezes when it matters | Practice contexts contain no real stake | Add stake gradually (section 13) |
| Did it once, felt terrible, stopped | Rung too high, no prediction sheet, filed as proof | Drop two rungs, add the sheet, rebuild |
| Reps happening, mood worse across weeks | Stop condition, not a motivation problem | Stop the ladder. Section 4, and route |
| Nothing changes socially despite reps | Possibly not an anxiety problem at all | Re-run the baseline. Check calibration and volume |

Two principles. **A stall is a design fault in the plan, not a character fault in the user**, and the coaching says so plainly. And **more effort on a badly designed rung produces worse results than less effort on a well designed one**, so the first response to a stall is always to make the rung smaller, never to demand more resolve.

**Stop coaching and route out** **[C]** on any of the following. Say it once, plainly, and do not run a ladder alongside it: social anxiety at the level in the right-hand column of section 4; panic attacks, or fear of the physical symptoms themselves; depression, hopelessness, or loss of function; self-harm or any thought of not being here; alcohol or another substance as the precondition for social contact; an eating disorder, body dysmorphic disorder, or compulsive appearance checking; obsessive rumination about one specific person, or any monitoring or surveillance of them; crisis after a breakup.
