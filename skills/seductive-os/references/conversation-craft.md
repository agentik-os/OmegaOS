# Conversation craft

What to say, and the structures underneath it. No scripts. Structures, one worked example, then the user's own words, per the SKILL output rules. A memorized line is detectable, it removes the user from the interaction, and it does not compound ([refusals.md](refusals.md) section 3).

## 1. The bottleneck is attention, not material

Almost every user who asks what to say has enough material and no attention. They arrive asking for topics, openers and lines, and the honest answer is that a person who is genuinely listening has more to say than they can use, because every sentence the other person produces contains three usable threads.

Test it before coaching anything else. Ask the user to describe their last conversation and name three specific things the other person said. If they cannot, the problem is upstream in [presence-and-embodiment.md](presence-and-embodiment.md) and no amount of conversational structure will help. Send them there first, then return.

The second most common misdiagnosis: the user has attention but is spending it on their own performance. Same fix, same document. Only once attention is outward does the craft in this file do anything.

## 2. Three layers, and depth as a pace

Conversation runs on three layers, and connection lives mostly on the third. The layer model is a craft device rather than a research finding **[E3]**: it is useful because it makes pacing visible, not because anyone has measured three layers.

| Layer | Content | Function | Failure when stuck here |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Facts** | Where you live, what you do, what happened | Establishes ground, safe, low cost | Interview. Two people exchanging a database |
| **Opinions** | What you think of it, what you would choose, what you avoid | Reveals a person, creates surface to react to | Debate. Positions without a person behind them |
| **Feelings and meaning** | What it was like, what you wanted, what you are unsure of | Where closeness happens | Intensity without ground. Reads as a demand |

Depth is a pace, not a level. The mistake is treating layer three as a destination to reach quickly. The working model is a ladder climbed together, one rung at a time, with a check after each:

```text
L0  logistics       is this seat taken, how do you know the host
L1  facts           what you did, where you have been, what happened
L2  opinions        what you think of it, what you would pick, what you avoid
L3  preference      what you like and dislike, with the reason left in
L4  feeling         what it was actually like, what you wanted, what worried you
L5  meaning         what you are building, what you regret, what you have not resolved
```

The rule: **step one rung past their last rung, then stop and see what they do.** If they match, continue. If they step back down, stay down for a while and try again later, or do not. Their pace is information, not resistance, and treating a step down as an obstacle is where this turns into pressure ([social-calibration.md](social-calibration.md) section 12).

## 3. The engine: reciprocal self-disclosure

The most robust finding available in this domain: mutual, escalating self-disclosure at a matched pace generates closeness, and the reciprocity itself matters, not just the volume **[E1]**. Structured closeness-generating procedures using escalating mutual disclosure reliably produce felt closeness between strangers in laboratory settings **[E1]**. How well that transfers to bars, apps and first dates is less established **[E2]**, so coach it as the engine and not as a guarantee.

What matters for coaching is the two failure modes, which are opposite and equally common:

**Disclosure ahead of the pace.** The user offers layer four or five material in the first twenty minutes. The other person now holds something heavy they did not ask for, and their choices are to reciprocate at a depth they have not chosen or to become a listener. Both feel like a demand. This is also how a trauma disclosure functions socially regardless of intent: it transfers a load. Not a character flaw, a calibration error, and it usually comes from wanting closeness fast.

**Disclosure behind the pace.** The user asks and never gives. The other person discloses, gets nothing back, and correctly concludes either that the user is concealing something or that this is an interview. Anxious users live here, because asking is safe and answering is exposure. The tell: the user can describe the other person in detail and the other person could not describe them at all.

Coaching move for both: match, then add one small increment. If they gave you a layer two opinion, give a layer two opinion plus one layer three reason. Then wait.

## 4. Question craft

Questions are a tool, not the conversation. Four rules do most of the work.

**Open versus closed.** Closed questions are fine and necessary; they are just not engines. Open questions produce material. The upgrade is usually one word: not "did you like it" but "what was it like".

**The follow-up that proves you listened.** The highest-value conversational move available for the effort it costs. There is direct evidence here rather than only craft: people who ask more questions, and follow-up questions in particular, tend to be better liked by their conversation partners, including in speed-dating settings **[E2]**. Treat the size of the effect as uncertain and the direction as sound. It requires nothing but attention: take the specific detail they just gave and ask about that, not about the category. Generic follow-ups signal that the answer was not heard.

```text
Them:  "I moved back from Lisbon in March, it was harder than I expected."
Generic follow-up:  "Oh nice, how long were you there?"        (returns to facts, discards the offer)
Real follow-up:     "Harder how?"                              (takes the offer, one rung up)
```

That worked example is illustrative only. Do not hand the user those words, hand them the shape: find the loaded word in what they said and ask about that word.

**The two-question rule.** After two questions in a row, say something of your own before the third. Three consecutive questions is an interview, and interviews make people feel evaluated rather than met. Track it as a ratio: over a conversation, roughly one disclosure for every question is a healthy shape.

**Specific over generic.** "What do you do" is a form field and gets a form answer. Questions that ask for a particular thing get real answers: what the best part of a described trip was, what they would change about a thing they just complained about, what they are currently annoyed by. Also useful: ask about the thing they clearly want to talk about, which they have usually already signalled twice.

One caution on "why": it is a strong question that occasionally puts people on the spot, especially about feelings. "What made you" and "how did that go" are gentler forms with the same yield.

## 5. Answering well

Half of question craft is answering, and it is the half users neglect.

- **Never just return it.** Answering "how was your week" with "good, you?" ends the exchange and hands the other person all the work. It is the most common conversational dead end there is.
- **Give something real first.** One specific detail, then optionally return the question. The detail is what they can grab.
- **Answer the layer they asked, plus a little.** A layer one question can be answered with a layer two response, and often should be.
- **Do not answer with a job title.** "What do you do" answered with a role is a dead end handed back. Answer with the part of it you have an opinion about, the strange part, or the part you are currently annoyed by.

```text
Them:  "So what do you do?"
Dead:  "I'm a project manager."
Alive: "I run builds for a hardware team, which is mostly telling people
        their timeline is fiction. Some weeks I'm right."
```

Again: the shape, not the sentence. The shape is fact plus a position plus a small self-implication.

## 6. One true thing

A standing practice, and one of the few in this pack that works from the first rep: **in every conversation, say one thing that is true and slightly costly.**

Not confession. Not the layer five disclosure. Costly means it could be disagreed with, or it reveals a preference, or it admits a small thing.

- an actual opinion about a thing under discussion, without hedging it into agreement
- a preference stated as a preference ("I would rather do almost anything than go to a club")
- a small admission ("I have no idea what that means")
- a real reaction in the moment ("that is genuinely the strangest job I have heard of")

Two effects. The other person learns there is a person here, which is what makes them able to like you specifically. And the user gets a small rep in tolerating exposure, which is the same muscle [rejection-resilience.md](rejection-resilience.md) trains at a larger scale. Score it as a process metric: did I say one true thing.

## 7. Statements as invitations

Users default to questions because questions feel safe and generous. They are neither, past a certain density. A well-made statement often outperforms a question because it does three things at once: it discloses, it gives the other person a surface to react to, and it does not oblige them to produce anything on demand.

| Question form | Statement form | What changes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Do you like living here?" | "I still cannot work out if I like living here." | Discloses, invites disagreement, no obligation |
| "What kind of music are you into?" | "I have decided I am too old for standing at gigs." | Gives a position to push against, is funnier, is honest |
| "Have you travelled much?" | "I have not left the country in three years and it has started to bother me." | Costs something, opens the real topic |

The mechanism is that a statement leaves the other person free. They can take it, refuse it, or turn it. A question requires an answer and the requirement is felt, which is why a stack of them reads as pressure even when it is meant as interest.

Mix. A conversation made only of statements is a broadcast, which is section 17's failure at a different address.

## 8. Being genuinely interesting

Interesting is not a performance skill and it cannot be faked for long. Three components, all trainable **[E3]**:

1. **Opinions with a reason attached.** An opinion with no reason is a flag. An opinion with a reason is a conversation. The user who agrees with everything is not being agreeable, they are being absent, and the absence is felt as blandness.
2. **Specifics over abstractions.** "I have been reading about X" is nothing. "I have been reading about X and the part that surprised me was Y" is something. Specifics are almost the entire technology of being interesting. Train the user to carry the detail, not the category.
3. **Actual positions.** Things they like, things they refuse, things they have decided about how they want to live. A person with no positions is difficult to be attracted to because there is nothing to be attracted to.

The upstream truth: interesting is downstream of a life with things in it. See [attraction-fundamentals.md](attraction-fundamentals.md) section 11. Conversational craft distributes what is there; it does not manufacture content from nothing, and users who feel they have nothing to say are sometimes right and need a different intervention.

## 9. Stories in conversation

Conversational storytelling is a different craft from written or stage storytelling, and the difference is length and handback. The structure below is practitioner knowledge **[E3]**, widely converged on by people who tell stories for a living and not experimentally established.

```text
HOOK    one sentence of situation. Start close to the interesting part
STAKE   what you wanted, feared, or had to do
TURN    the thing that went sideways
LAND    the image, the line, the reaction. One beat, not three
STOP    hand it back before the natural end
```

Four rules:

- **Short.** Past roughly sixty to ninety seconds without a handback, it stops being conversation and becomes a performance, and the other person's job changes from participant to audience. They notice the change even if they cannot name it.
- **Concrete.** One image beats a summary. Say what was on the table.
- **Self-implicating.** The user should be the one who looks foolish, wrong or human. Stories where the teller is the hero read as a bid for rank, which is section 8 of [attraction-fundamentals.md](attraction-fundamentals.md).
- **End early.** Land it and stop. Every added clarification lowers the landing.

The length failure is the most common and the user cannot detect it from inside. Get them to notice the physical signal instead: when the other person's contributions shrink to nods, the story ended two minutes ago.

## 10. Humour that builds, humour that costs

Humour is high leverage and highly personal **[P]**. There is no universal funny, and a user should be funny in their own register rather than borrowing one. What is generalizable is which structures tend to build connection and which tend to cost it **[E3]**.

| Builds | Why |
| --- | --- |
| Shared observation about the situation you are both in | Creates a "we", costs nobody anything, always available |
| Light self-deprecation | Signals security, releases pressure. Light is the operative word |
| Playful exaggeration of something already established | Requires listening, which is why it lands |
| The callback | Proof of attention in comic form, one of the strongest moves available |
| Absurd agreement, running with their premise instead of correcting it | Collaborative, gives them somewhere to go |

| Costs | Why |
| --- | --- |
| Sarcasm at their expense | Reads as contempt more often than intended, and cannot be un-read |
| The anecdote as performance | Turns a conversation into a set. See section 9 |
| Jokes that need permission, checked with a look afterwards | The check is the tell, and it transfers the discomfort to them |
| Heavy self-deprecation | Stops being funny and becomes a request for reassurance, which they must now supply |
| Punching at anyone in the group who cannot punch back | Everyone present updates on you, not on the target |

Coaching note: a user who is not naturally funny should not be trained to be funny. Warmth and attention outperform manufactured humour, and someone visibly trying to be funny is worse off than someone who is simply interested.

## 11. Teasing, and the exact line at negging

Teasing done well is one of the fastest ways to move a conversation from polite to alive. Negging is a refused tactic ([refusals.md](refusals.md) section 3). They are not on a spectrum, they are different operations, and the line is precise:

| | Teasing | Negging |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Target | Something incidental, or something they are secure about | Something they are insecure about |
| Purpose | Play. Both people are up | Create a deficit so approval becomes valuable |
| Return | Fully returnable, and the user enjoys receiving it | Not returnable, that is the point |
| Effect if it lands | Both laugh, the temperature rises | They lose a little footing |
| After a flinch | Stop immediately, name it lightly, move on | Continue, because the flinch was the goal |

Three operating rules:

1. **Tease the choice, not the person.** Their indefensible coffee order, their loyalty to a bad team, the confidence with which they gave you wrong directions. Never their body, their accent, their job, their income, their intelligence, or anything they have just told you they are self-conscious about.
2. **The reversal test.** If they teased you back with exactly that shape, would it land warmly? If it would sting, do not send it.
3. **Stop at the first flinch, and repair small.** A flat "no, that was a rubbish joke, sorry" and a move on. Do not build a production around the repair; that makes them manage your discomfort about their discomfort.

A user who has to ask whether a specific line is teasing or negging has usually answered their own question. When in doubt, be warm and specific instead: a real observation about something they said produces the same "this person is not just flattering me" signal with none of the cost.

## 12. Disagreement as connection

Disagreement, held warmly, is one of the most underused connective moves available. It signals that the user is not performing agreement to be liked, which is exactly the signal that most needs sending, and it gives the other person something real to engage.

The shape: state the disagreement plainly, give the reason, stay curious, and do not need to win.

```text
"I think that is completely wrong, and I want to know why you think it,
 because you are clearly not stupid about this."
```

Structure, not a line. The three components are: a clear position, warmth that is visible in the delivery, and genuine curiosity about their reasoning.

Failure modes: contrarianism as a personality (disagreeing with everything is agreement with nothing, and reads as a bid for attention), needing to win (the moment the user needs the point, connection stops), and the debate register (volume, speed, interruption, which section 5 of [presence-and-embodiment.md](presence-and-embodiment.md) covers). Being wrong cleanly, once, without collapsing is the rarest and most attractive version of this skill.

## 13. The lull

Lulls are normal, and the panic is the problem rather than the silence. A user who can sit in a three-second gap without visible discomfort is already ahead.

Three moves, in order of quality:

1. **Return to something they said earlier.** "You said earlier that X, and I have been wondering about it since." This is the callback in serious register, and it is the strongest move because it is proof of attention.
2. **Say what you notice.** About the room, the event, the situation you are both in. Shared observation, always available, costs nothing.
3. **Offer something about yourself.** A layer two or three statement. Section 7.

What not to do: refill the silence with a question, which converts the lull into an interview; announce the lull ("well, this got awkward"), which makes it their problem; or produce filler at speed, which is the anxious tell.

And the honest fourth option: some lulls mean the conversation is finished. That is a normal, non-catastrophic outcome, and reading it correctly is [social-calibration.md](social-calibration.md), section 7. Ending a finished conversation warmly is a skill, not a failure.

## 14. Conversational generosity

The unifying frame: give the other person somewhere to go. Every move in this document can be checked against it.

- **Ask what they can actually answer.** Enormous questions ("what are you passionate about") are work. Small specific questions are gifts.
- **Set them up to be interesting.** If they mentioned a thing they clearly like, hand them the floor on it and be genuinely interested in the answer.
- **Take the thread they offered.** People signal what they want to talk about, usually twice. Ignoring it in favour of your own thread is the most common form of not listening.
- **Do not correct trivia.** Being right about a date, a name or a statistic costs more than it returns, every time, unless it matters.
- **Do not top their story.** The reflex to answer a story with a bigger one converts a conversation into a contest. Ask about theirs instead.
- **Leave room.** Talk less than half. Users badly overestimate their share; the honest way to find out is to ask a friend.

## 15. Ending it well

End while it is still good. A conversation that runs until it dies leaves both people with the dying part as the memory, and users stay too long almost exclusively out of anxiety about the ending.

```text
1  MARK      "I should get back to my friends" / "I need to head off"
2  NAME      one specific thing you liked about the conversation, not a generic compliment
3  ASK       if you want something further, say so plainly, once, easy to decline
4  LEAVE     actually leave. Do not linger through the exit
```

Step three is where [ethics-and-consent.md](ethics-and-consent.md) section 9 governs: specific, owned, low-cost to decline, answer-tolerant. Step four is where most users fail, hovering after the ask, which converts a clean invitation into pressure. Leave whether the answer was yes or no, at the same speed, with the same face. That symmetry is the actual skill, and it is what makes the next person's no cheap to give.

Anything past making interest legible belongs to [flirtation-and-escalation.md](flirtation-and-escalation.md).

## 16. Groups

Group conversation is a different game and most users are worse at it than they think.

- **Address the group, not one person.** Eye contact that circulates while speaking, including to the quiet ones. Talking to one person inside a group of five excludes four people who all noticed.
- **Take the floor once, then hand it back.** Deliberately pass to someone: "you were there for that, what actually happened?" This is the highest-status move available in a group and it costs nothing.
- **Bring in the person on the edge.** Ask them something small and specific. Everyone present registers it.
- **Do not fight for airtime.** Speed and volume competition loses even when it wins. Wait, then speak into the gap at normal pace.
- **Never separate someone from their group.** This is a refused move for a reason ([refusals.md](refusals.md) tier 1). If the conversation should continue elsewhere, they can suggest it, or it can happen another day.
- **Read the friend group.** They are participants, not obstacles, and they are usually the fastest source of accurate signal ([social-calibration.md](social-calibration.md) section 9).

## 17. The thing that ruins most conversations

Planning your next line while they talk.

It is the single largest quality drain in this document, it is invisible to the user and completely visible to the other person, and it produces the specific dead texture where each person's turn does not connect to the previous one. It also destroys calibration, because the attention that should be reading them is drafting instead.

The mechanism is not mysterious. Planning speech and comprehending speech draw on overlapping limited capacity, and performance on one degrades when the other is loaded **[E2]**. A user who is drafting is, quite literally, listening with less. That is the same finding underneath section 1, arriving at the same conclusion from the other direction.

The correction is a permission, not a technique:

```text
You are allowed two seconds of silence after they finish.
You are required to have nothing prepared when they do.
```

That is the whole intervention, and it is hard. Drill it as a count: how many times in one conversation did you have nothing ready when your turn came, and say something anyway. Users report that the sentences produced this way are better than the drafted ones, which is the point.

## 18. Practice ladder

Deliberate practice, explicitly not approaching. None of the rungs below are romantic, and rungs 1 to 4 should never be used as cover for an approach ([ethics-and-consent.md](ethics-and-consent.md) section 7 on service settings). The ladder is craft **[E3]**: graded exposure with a specific target per rep is the shape that works in practice, and no one has run a trial on conversational reps.

```text
1  Transactional      one full sentence with staff, cashiers, drivers. Not an approach.
2  Ambient            a shared observation with someone in a queue, a lift, a waiting room
3  Structured         a class, a club, a league, a volunteer shift. Repeat contact, low stakes
4  Colleagues         someone at work you do not know well. Two questions, one disclosure
5  Friends of friends at a gathering. The real training ground: safe, repeated, consequential
6  Depth              take an existing friendship one rung deeper (L3 to L4). Hardest, most useful
```

Rung 5 is where most of the learning lives, and rung 6 is the one users skip. Deepening an existing relationship trains exactly the same disclosure pacing that section 3 describes, with a person who already likes you, which makes it the safest possible place to be bad at it.

Structure a first real conversation with [first_conversation.md](../protocols/first_conversation.md), debrief every rep with [interaction_debrief.md](../protocols/interaction_debrief.md), and schedule the week with [weekly_practice.md](../protocols/weekly_practice.md).

## 19. Practice tiers

| Tier | Contents |
| --- | --- |
| **Floor** | One conversation of any kind, with one true thing said in it. Log three specifics they said |
| **Standard** | Floor most days, plus two rung 3 to 5 reps per week, plus one deliberate follow-up-question rep per conversation, plus one debrief |
| **Deep** | One rung 6 conversation (deepen an existing relationship), one recorded self-review of talk-time share, one conversation ended deliberately early while still good |

One conversational practice per cycle. A user drilling five structures at once is executing rather than listening, which is section 17 in a new outfit.

## 20. Diagnosis

| The user says | Likely actual cause | Route |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "I run out of things to say" | Not listening. Every answer contains threads | Section 1, then [presence-and-embodiment.md](presence-and-embodiment.md) |
| "It stays on the surface" | Nobody stepped up a rung, usually the user | Sections 2 and 3 |
| "They stopped replying to me" | Interview shape, or no disclosure to attach to | Sections 4 and 5 |
| "I talk too much" | Anxiety filling silence, or no handback in stories | Sections 8, 9 and 17 |
| "I am boring" | No positions, or an actual content problem | Section 8, then [attraction-fundamentals.md](attraction-fundamentals.md) |
| "It gets intense too fast" | Disclosure ahead of the pace | Section 3 |
| "I make them uncomfortable and I do not know when" | Teasing off target, or missed step-downs | Sections 11 and [social-calibration.md](social-calibration.md) |
| "I can talk to anyone but nothing goes anywhere" | Performing, no reception, or interest never made legible | Section 17, then [ethics-and-consent.md](ethics-and-consent.md) section 9 |

The last row is the most misdiagnosed in the pack. Fluent conversation with no outcome is usually not a conversation problem at all: it is an unstated interest problem, and the fix is legibility, not better material.

## 21. Route out

Conversational difficulty is ordinarily trainable and most of it belongs here. Some of it does not. Label **[C]** and route to a qualified professional via [safety-and-boundaries.md](safety-and-boundaries.md) when the user reports:

- speech that shuts down entirely under social pressure, mutism, or freezing they cannot interrupt;
- avoidance of speaking that restricts work, study or daily life, rather than making it uncomfortable;
- rumination after conversations that lasts for days, or replaying single sentences for weeks;
- distressing intrusive material arriving uninvited in ordinary conversation, which is a support question and not a pacing question ([attraction-fundamentals.md](attraction-fundamentals.md) section 10);
- a stammer, selective mutism, or another speech difference the user wants clinical help with. Speech and language therapy is a real profession and conversational coaching is not a substitute for it.

Below that threshold, ordinary fear of speaking is trained here and in [anxiety-and-avoidance.md](anxiety-and-avoidance.md). Do not pathologise nerves, and do not coach through something that needs care.
