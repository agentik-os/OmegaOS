## 1. Identity doctrine

Identity is a revisable model that organizes attention and behavior. It is not a mystical switch and should not become a prison.

Build identity from:

- chosen meaning;
- values under tradeoff;
- roles and responsibilities;
- observable standards;
- repeated evidence;
- honest repair after violations.

Avoid absolute labels such as “unstoppable,” “always disciplined,” or “born billionaire.” They create brittleness and invite self-deception.

## 2. Purpose stack

Define purpose at five levels:

| Level | Prompt |
| --- | --- |
| Existence | What makes a life worth living to you? |
| Contribution | Whose life, work, or world should improve because you existed? |
| Craft | What capabilities or forms of work deserve mastery? |
| Season | What is this chapter of life asking you to build, heal, learn, or release? |
| Today | What action would embody that purpose before the day ends? |

Purpose may be discovered, chosen, inherited, co-created, or spiritually received. Do not force a single grand statement.

## 3. Why ladder

Start with the explicit goal and ask “Why does that matter?” five to seven times. At each level test:

- Is this intrinsically desired or socially borrowed?
- Is this about moving toward something or escaping something?
- Would it still matter without applause?
- What emotion does the user expect the outcome to create?
- Can part of that emotion or value be lived now?
- What responsibility comes with receiving the outcome?

End with:

- **Why:** the life-giving reason.
- **Anti-why:** the future pain or regret to avoid.
- **For whom:** beneficiaries and responsibilities.
- **Without sacrificing:** explicit guardrails.

## 4. Values and anti-values

Select three to five operational values. Force tradeoffs with paired choices:

- freedom vs certainty;
- mastery vs variety;
- truth vs approval;
- ambition vs presence;
- speed vs craftsmanship;
- privacy vs visibility;
- service vs status;
- autonomy vs belonging.

For each chosen value define:

```text
Value: Freedom
Means: Control of time, location, attention, and commitments.
Looks like: Owning systems and saying no to misaligned obligations.
Does not mean: Avoiding all structure or responsibility.
Tradeoff: Accepting short-term constraint to build durable assets.
Violation signal: Calendar full of low-value commitments chosen from fear.
Repair: Cancel, delegate, renegotiate, or time-box one obligation within 48h.
```

Also define anti-values: deception, dependence, vanity, chaos, domination, neglect, or any user-chosen pattern to refuse.

## 5. Identity specification

Create an `Identity Spec` with these fields:

### Name and season

Use a grounded title, such as “Gareth — Builder-Steward Season,” not a fictional alter ego unless the user wants one.

### Roles

Examples: human being, person of faith, partner/friend, founder, creator, athlete, learner, steward, citizen.

### Five identity principles

Each principle must imply a decision. Example: “Truth before ego: I seek customer evidence before expanding the vision.”

### Behavioral standards

Write standards in `When X, I do Y` form:

- When overwhelmed, I select one outcome and park the rest.
- When I make a mistake, I disclose, repair, learn, and update the system.
- When money arrives, I allocate before lifestyle expands.
- When tired, I protect the floor instead of performing the full routine.
- When opportunity conflicts with integrity, I decline it.

### Boundaries

Define what the identity does not do: deceptive selling, performative spending, sleep sacrifice as default, disrespect, revenge work, compulsive comparison, or unsafe optimization.

### Evidence ledger

Track small “identity votes” with date, context, action, difficulty, and lesson. Do not track only perfect days.

## 6. Belief architecture

Classify beliefs:

- **Core:** worth, safety, lovability, agency.
- **Identity:** “I am the kind of person who…”
- **Rule:** “To succeed, I must…”
- **Prediction:** “If I sell, they will reject me.”
- **Meaning:** “A failed launch proves…”
- **Possibility:** “People like me can/cannot…”

For each limiting belief:

1. State it precisely.
2. Identify origin and protective function.
3. Separate fact from interpretation.
4. Gather supporting and disconfirming evidence.
5. Replace with a credible bridge belief.
6. Design a small behavioral experiment.
7. Update from the result.

Bridge belief example:

- Brittle: “Everything I touch becomes successful.”
- Credible: “I can learn to run smaller tests, tolerate rejection, and improve my odds.”

## 7. Self-trust loop

```text
MAKE SMALL HONEST PROMISE
  -> KEEP IT OR REPAIR QUICKLY
    -> RECORD EVIDENCE
      -> INCREASE CAPACITY GRADUALLY
        -> MAKE LARGER PROMISE
```

Self-trust is not confidence that every outcome will work. It is confidence that the person will face reality, act, learn, and repair.

## 8. Identity exercises

Use selectively:

- future-self letter from 3 years ahead;
- anti-vision: a normal day in 10 years if nothing changes;
- eulogy or legacy reflection;
- regret-minimization test;
- ideal ordinary Tuesday, not only peak experiences;
- role-model decomposition: qualities, behaviors, costs, shadow side;
- “not me / no longer me / becoming me / already me” matrix;
- identity evidence timeline;
- 80th-birthday voices from loved ones, team, customers, and self;
- enoughness statement: what money is for and when more stops improving the target life;
- deathbed and Monday-morning dual test: inspiring at the end, executable at the start.

Never use future-self exercises to shame the present self. The present self is the builder, not the enemy.
