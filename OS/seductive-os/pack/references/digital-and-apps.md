# Digital and apps

Apps, profiles, openers, texting and the whole mediated layer. Everything here inherits [ethics-and-consent.md](ethics-and-consent.md), and section 12 of that document (digital consent) is the operative part: the medium changes the cost structure, not the rules.

This is the most technically fixable area in the pack. A large share of what users experience as personal failure on the apps is a solvable formatting problem sitting on top of a base rate nobody told them about.

## 1. What apps are good at, and what they are bad at

State both halves before doing any optimization work, because a user who believes apps are the main event will misread everything that follows.

| Good at | Bad at |
| --- | --- |
| Volume. More new people in a week than most lives produce in a year | Signal. A thumbnail and a paragraph predict almost nothing about compatibility |
| Reaching outside the user's social graph entirely | Context. Nobody is seen behaving over time, which is what the ranking in [dating-strategy.md](dating-strategy.md) is built on |
| Filtering on stated hard requirements (geography, wanting children, structure) | Filtering on the things that actually matter (kindness under stress, how conflict goes, whether you laugh) |
| Finding people who are explicitly available and looking | The psychological cost of being evaluated as a thumbnail, repeatedly, by strangers |
| Reaching small or dispersed communities that are hard to find offline | Producing anything resembling repeated exposure **[E1]**, which is the mechanism the best offline contexts run on |

The honest positioning: apps are a legitimate and often necessary supplement, they are third-ranked on yield per unit of effort, and they carry a psychological cost that the offline contexts do not. Use them deliberately, in seasons, alongside recurring social surface. Never as the entire strategy.

## 2. Base rates, and why a low match rate is structural

Say this early and plainly to any user who is demoralized: **the match rate is a property of the market, not a verdict on the person** **[E3]**.

The structural facts, without inventing numbers:

- The ratio of people looking to people being looked at is heavily skewed on most platforms, and the skew differs sharply by platform, by orientation, by city and by demographic. Two people can run identical profiles on the same app and see completely different volumes for reasons that have nothing to do with either of them.
- The interface is designed for rapid rejection, at speed, with almost no information. Most passes are not judgements about a person, they are the mechanical output of a sorting interface.
- Engagement is the platform's product, not matching. An app that solved matching for a user would lose them, and it is worth saying so once, without conspiracy, as a reason to keep the app in perspective.
- Non-response is the modal outcome for almost everyone. It is not data about the user's worth, and it is only weak data about the user's profile.

The coaching consequence: fix the fixable (photos, format, targeting), then measure over a season rather than over an afternoon, and never let the app supply the user's evidence about themselves. If the app is supplying that evidence, the work is in [inner-game-and-self-worth.md](inner-game-and-self-worth.md) and the correct prescription may be to delete it.

## 3. The profile as a filter, not a maximizer

The choice that determines everything else. A profile optimized for maximum matches and a profile optimized for the right matches are different objects, and users almost always build the first while wanting the second.

```text
MAXIMIZER                        FILTER
broad, agreeable, inoffensive    specific, opinionated, particular
appeals to everyone              appeals to fewer, and to the right fewer
more matches                     fewer matches, better conversations
more first dates                 fewer first dates, more second ones
nothing to talk about            three obvious things to talk about
```

The maximizer profile produces the exact experience users complain about: matches that go nowhere, conversations with no purchase, dates with a stranger who turns out to want something entirely different. Every specific detail added is a filter that does work for free, before anyone has typed anything.

Practical rule: a profile should make some people not swipe. If nobody would rule the user out, the profile says nothing. This is **[P]** in content and **[E3]** in structure: what to be specific about is the user's business, that they be specific is the craft.

## 4. Photos: the highest-leverage fix on the apps

Say it plainly, because it is true and users resist it: **photos are the single highest-leverage change available on the apps**, and most people are failing at them technically rather than aesthetically **[E3]**.

| Rule | Reason |
| --- | --- |
| A clear, unobstructed face in the first photo | It is the one question the first photo has to answer. Anything blocking it wastes the only guaranteed impression |
| Natural light, outdoors or by a window | Overhead indoor light and phone flash are unflattering to everyone. This is physics, not appearance |
| Recent and real, within about a year | Misrepresentation is a disclosure violation (section 15) and it produces an unpleasant first meeting for both people |
| Variety of contexts: face, full body, doing something, with people | Each answers a different question. Five near-identical photos answer one question five times |
| At least one full-body photo | Its absence is read as concealment, which is worse than whatever the user imagines they are hiding |
| At least one showing what they actually do | The best conversation starter available, and it filters for shared interest |
| No group photo first | Nobody solves a puzzle. If they cannot tell which person is the user in two seconds, they move on |
| No sunglasses first, no hat obscuring the face first | Same reason as the first row. Later in the set, fine |
| No heavy filters, no beauty smoothing | Detectable, and it reads as insecurity rather than as attractiveness |
| No mirror selfie as the lead | Weak framing, bad light, and it signals nobody was there to take a photo. Fine deeper in the set |
| Smiling in at least one, genuinely | A relaxed face is easier to approach. A serious face in every photo reads as unavailable |
| Not exclusively photos with an ex cropped out | Visible, and it is a small honesty problem |

Getting them: a friend, a phone, thirty minutes in daylight, forty photos in three locations, two changes of clothing, keep six. That is the entire production, and it beats a professional shoot, which tends to produce images that look nothing like the person who arrives.

## 5. Getting real feedback on photos

The user cannot judge their own photos. Neither can anyone who loves them. Structure the feedback so honesty is the easy answer **[E3]**:

- **Ask for a rank order, never a yes or no.** "Put these eight in order" produces honesty that "is this one okay" cannot.
- **Ask a specific question:** which one would you talk to, which one looks least like me, which one looks like I am trying too hard.
- **Ask several people separately**, then look for agreement across three. One opinion is taste, three matching opinions is signal.
- **Ask people who are not in the user's dating pool and not invested in their feelings.** Friends who are the demographic the user is hoping to meet are useful precisely because they are used to reading these images.
- **Watch behaviour rather than opinions where possible.** Which photo people react to, which one gets mentioned unprompted.
- **Discard reassurance.** Anyone whose feedback is only ever kind is out of the sample, warmly.

Photo work also overlaps with [style-and-self-presentation.md](style-and-self-presentation.md). Fit and condition show up in photographs even more than in person, because a still image gives the viewer time.

## 6. The written profile

Short, specific, and answerable. Its job is to give a real person a real reason to type something.

- **Specific over general.** A named thing beats a category, every time. Categories are invisible because everyone lists the same ones.
- **A real opinion.** Something with a position in it. Mild, low-stakes and genuinely held is enough. It gives people something to agree or disagree with, which is a conversation.
- **Something answerable.** At least one line that has an obvious reply attached to it. This does more for the opener problem (section 8) than anything the other person could learn about openers.
- **Humour that is actually the user's.** Borrowed jokes and template bits are recognizable, and they set a register the user then has to maintain in person.
- **One line on what they are looking for**, stated plainly. It filters, it saves both people time, and it is the single most under-used line on most profiles ([dating-strategy.md](dating-strategy.md) section 11).
- **Length: short.** A few lines. A long profile reads as a CV and gets skimmed to nothing.
- **Written in the user's own voice**, which means it should sound like how they talk, not like how profiles sound.

## 7. The sentences that carry zero information

Not refused, just useless. They occupy the space where a filter should be, and they are interchangeable across millions of profiles.

`Love to laugh` · `Work hard, play hard` · `Looking for my partner in crime` · `Fluent in sarcasm` · `Just ask` · `Not good at these` · `Adventure seeker` · `Live life to the fullest` · `Ask me anything` · `Foodie` · `Love travelling` (with no specifics) · `Good vibes only` · `No drama` · lists of adjectives with no evidence · a height stated as a filter with a complaint attached · anything about what the user does not want, in a list.

The correction is mechanical: replace each with the specific version of the same claim. The specific version is shorter, more interesting, and does the filtering the generic one was pretending to do.

One category to name separately: negative framing (rules, exclusions, complaints about the app or about previous matches). It reads as recent bad experience rather than as standards, and it is one of the few things that reliably costs a user matches they would have wanted.

## 8. The opener

Short, specific, answerable. That is the entire craft.

- **Reference something specific from their profile.** It proves the message was written for them, which is the only thing an opener actually has to prove.
- **Ask something answerable.** Not an interrogation, one question with an obvious hook. A message with no question is a message that requires the other person to do all the work.
- **Keep it short.** A paragraph in an opener is a lot to receive, and it raises the cost of not replying.
- **Match the register of the platform and of their profile.** Playful profile, playful opener. Sincere profile, sincere opener.
- **Nothing about their body.** They are a stranger. See [flirtation-and-escalation.md](flirtation-and-escalation.md) section 8.
- **Never a copy-paste at volume.** Refused in [refusals.md](refusals.md): it treats people as a funnel, it is detectable, and it is an efficient machine for generating the exact non-response that was already the user's bottleneck. Twenty considered messages beat four hundred copies on every metric, including total matches.
- **One message, then wait.** No reply is an answer. A second message is pressure, and it is the clearest early signal of someone who does not read answers ([ethics-and-consent.md](ethics-and-consent.md) section 12).

## 9. From conversation to meeting

Long text relationships fail, reliably, and knowing why prevents the most common way a good match evaporates **[E3]**. Two mechanisms: both people build a version of the other that the real meeting has to compete with, and the effort of sustaining the text exchange gets spent instead of being spent on meeting.

The move: after a small number of real exchanges, suggest something specific, short and low-key, and make it easy to decline. Give a concrete option rather than "we should meet sometime", which is the text equivalent of never asking.

Handle the answers correctly. A yes with a time is a yes. A yes with no time offered, twice, is a no with better manners. An explicit not yet, with a reason (nervous, wants a video call first, prefers to text a while), is a legitimate answer and should be met with an easy agreement and no sulking. Some people genuinely need longer, and the difference between them and a permanent text pen pal is that they eventually offer a time.

## 10. Texting between meetings

Match the pace, and stop engineering it.

- **Reply when you see it and want to.** Deliberate delay, the three-day rule, and strategic thinning are refused ([refusals.md](refusals.md)): they are small lies about interest, and they are the most common way people lose someone they actually wanted.
- **Match their rhythm.** Length, frequency and register. Sustained mismatch is information about compatibility rather than a problem to solve.
- **One clean message maximum after no reply**, then stop. Not a barrage, not a passive-aggressive check-in, not a meme to reopen the channel.
- **Do not run the relationship over text.** Logistics, warmth and continuity, yes. Serious conversations, conflict, and anything requiring a face belong in person or on a call.
- **Do not screenshot and crowdsource.** The other person is not in the room and did not consent to a panel. Occasional advice from one close friend is normal, running every message past a group chat is not, and it replaces the user's own calibration with a committee's.
- **If the user's texting anxiety is running the show** (drafting for twenty minutes, checking read receipts, reading tone into punctuation), that is an inner-game problem, not a texting problem. Route to [inner-game-and-self-worth.md](inner-game-and-self-worth.md) and [anxiety-and-avoidance.md](anxiety-and-avoidance.md).

## 11. Sexting and images

Simple rules, stated without embarrassment, because vagueness here causes real harm.

- **Only when clearly mutual, and preferably requested.** Escalation to explicit content follows the same reciprocity rule as everything else: met, not tolerated ([flirtation-and-escalation.md](flirtation-and-escalation.md) section 4).
- **Unsolicited sexual images are a violation.** Refused in every framing, and criminal in many jurisdictions. There is no version of this the OS assists with.
- **Permanence is the part people underestimate.** Anything sent can be screenshotted, saved, backed up, forwarded, and can outlive the relationship and the platform. Say this plainly once, to both halves of the situation: what the user sends, and what they receive.
- **What is received is held in confidence, permanently.** Forwarding, showing, or keeping images after a relationship ends against the other person's wishes is a betrayal, and distributing them is a crime in many places.
- **A user can decline** at any point, for any reason, including after previously participating. Pressure inside a sexting exchange is pressure ([safety-and-boundaries.md](safety-and-boundaries.md)).
- **Faces, identifying tattoos, and recognizable rooms** are a practical consideration worth mentioning once, neutrally, without lecturing.

## 12. Ghosting, from both sides

**When the user is ghosted:** it is common, it is mostly about the other person's avoidance rather than about the user, and the correct response is one clean message at most and then nothing. No investigation, no second account, no arriving somewhere they will be. The absence of an explanation is genuinely painful and it is also not owed. Run [post_rejection_debrief.md](../protocols/post_rejection_debrief.md) and route to [rejection-resilience.md](rejection-resilience.md).

**When the user is tempted to ghost:** the OS's position is that a short, kind, non-negotiating message is better, costs about fifteen seconds, and leaves the other person intact ([dating-strategy.md](dating-strategy.md) section 15). After a single unremarkable app conversation, silence is a normal and acceptable norm and nobody is wronged. After meetings have happened, send the message.

**The exception, again, is safety.** Where the user feels unsafe, has been harassed, or expects escalation, silence and a block are correct and require no justification. Never coach a frightened user into a closing conversation.

## 13. Safety, for both people

The same list as [dating-strategy.md](dating-strategy.md) section 7, restated here because it is on the apps that it gets skipped: a video call before meeting if either person wants one, a public first meeting, a friend told where and when, own transport both ways, own drink and own pace.

Two additional app-specific points. **Verify the person exists as described:** a video call, or a photo of something specific asked for in the moment, settles it without accusation, and anyone genuine will not object. And **pressure to skip any of the above is itself the finding**. A match who resists a video call, pushes for a private first meeting, wants to move to another platform immediately, or treats safety steps as an insult has given the user real information. Coach users to notice it and to disengage without argument.

## 14. Catfishing, scams and romance fraud

Brief and practical. The markers, which cluster **[E3]**:

- Very fast intensity: declarations of strong feeling within days, before anything is known.
- Persistent refusal or failure of video calls, always with a plausible reason.
- Photos that look professional or inconsistent, and an identity that does not survive a reverse image search.
- A biography with a built-in reason for absence and for financial irregularity: overseas contracts, an oil rig, deployment, international business.
- Immediate pressure to move off the platform to a private channel.
- Eventually, money. An emergency, a customs fee, a medical bill, a locked account, or an investment opportunity that the user is uniquely lucky to hear about. Crypto investment framing is now at least as common as the emergency framing.
- Escalating requests, each small, each urgent, each following a previous compliance.

The rules: **never send money to someone not met in person**, no exceptions and no matter how coherent the story. Never send explicit images to an unverified person (sextortion follows this route). Reverse image search is free and takes seconds. And if a user is currently in one of these, they are being victimized by a professional, they are not stupid, and shame is the mechanism that keeps people in it. Say that, then route them to the platform's reporting tools and to local fraud reporting.

## 15. Disclosure rules

Non-negotiable, from the spine:

- **No impersonation.** Never a photo that is not the user, never someone else's identity, never a fabricated name used to deceive rather than the ordinary privacy practice of withholding a surname.
- **No misrepresenting relationship status.** Partnered users on apps disclose the structure they have actually agreed. Negotiated non-monogamy is supported without commentary. Concealed infidelity is not, and the OS does not help build cover ([ethics-and-consent.md](ethics-and-consent.md) section 13).
- **No misrepresenting intent.** Stating a search for a relationship in order to obtain something casual, or the reverse, is deception about the thing the other person's consent is actually based on.
- **No materially false facts** about age, occupation, location, or whether the user has children.
- **Photos that are actually them, actually recent.** The most common small deception, and the one most reliably discovered at the worst moment.

Withholding is not lying. Users are entitled to privacy about their surname, employer, address and history until they choose otherwise (section 17). The line is between not yet telling and actively misrepresenting.

## 16. Burnout and the delete-and-return cycle

Legitimate, healthy, and worth normalizing rather than treating as a discipline failure.

App burnout is produced by continuous exposure to rapid evaluation, and the symptoms are recognizable: dread on opening the app, swiping without reading, describing people as categories, relief at a cancellation, a mood that tracks the notification count. None of that is fixed by better photos.

The seasonal pattern from [dating-strategy.md](dating-strategy.md) section 18 applies directly: apps on for a defined season, then fully deleted for a real break while the offline recurring activities continue. Deleting is not quitting, and it should be framed as a scheduled part of the strategy rather than as a failure. Notifications off during a season is also a legitimate middle setting, because it moves the app from interrupting the user to being visited by them.

## 17. Privacy, and the research boundary

Two directions, both mattering.

**What the user puts up:** no workplace name plus first name plus neighbourhood together, no home landmarks, no photos of other people (especially children) without consent, no linked accounts that expose a surname, and awareness that a face on a dating app is searchable. This is basic hygiene, not paranoia, and it protects the user in exactly the situations they most need protecting.

**What the user looks up:** do not research a match beyond what they offered ([ethics-and-consent.md](ethics-and-consent.md) section 12). A reverse image search for verification is proportionate safety. Reading an employer's staff page, an old social account, a family, or a home address before a first meeting is surveillance, and it reliably surfaces in conversation as knowledge the user should not have. It also destroys the actual point of the meeting, which is meeting them.

## 18. Running the profile audit

Full procedure in [digital_profile_audit.md](../protocols/digital_profile_audit.md). What it needs:

```text
INPUTS
  screenshots     the profile exactly as it appears to others, all photos, in order
  the numbers     matches per week, conversations started, meetings arranged
                  (as a rate over a season, never over one bad day)
  platform mix    which apps, and whether they suit what the user says they want
  targeting       who the profile is written for, stated out loud
  history         last 5 openers sent, last 5 conversations that died, and where
  short list      from dating-strategy section 5
OUTPUT, in this order and no other
  1. photo set fix        the highest-leverage change, almost always
  2. one specificity fix  a filter added where a generic line was
  3. one opener fix       specific, answerable, short
  4. one transition fix   how and when a meeting gets proposed
  5. one boundary fix     safety, privacy, or reply behaviour
  everything else to NOT NOW
```

Measure over a season. Changing four things at once teaches nothing, and app-level noise will swamp a two-day comparison.

## 19. Refused here

- **Mass messaging and copy-paste at volume.** Refused ([refusals.md](refusals.md)). It treats people as throughput and manufactures the rejection the user least tolerates.
- **Unsolicited sexual images.** Refused in every framing.
- **Fake photos, fake identity, fake biography, borrowed lifestyle props.** Refused. It obtains interest under false pretences and comes due.
- **Concealing relationship status or intent.** Refused.
- **Engineered delays, read-receipt games, the three-day rule.** Refused as manipulation and corrected as ineffective.
- **A second and third message into silence.** Refused. One unanswered message is an answer.
- **Researching a match beyond what they offered.** Refused as surveillance.
- **Screenshotting or forwarding private messages and images.** Refused. The person is not in the room.
- **Automation: bots, auto-swipers, generated messages sent at scale.** Refused. It is the funnel error with better tooling, and it degrades the space for everyone in it.
