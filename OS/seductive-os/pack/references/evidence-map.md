# Evidence map

## 1. Interpretation rules

- An average effect across a population says close to nothing about one person in one interaction. Attraction research reports small to moderate effects across large samples, and any individual case is dominated by variance the studies do not capture.
- Association is not causation, and most of this field is correlational.
- A finding from a laboratory or a speed-dating event may not transfer to a bar, an app, a workplace or a five-year relationship.
- A useful practice does not need an experiment behind it. It needs an honest label.
- The reverse also holds: a study behind a practice does not make the practice appropriate, ethical, or applicable to the user in front of you.
- When a claim would change what a user does with another person, the burden of evidence is higher, not lower.
- Where the honest answer is "nobody knows, and it depends on the two people", say that. It is a real answer and users can tell it apart from evasion.

## 2. How sources are cited here, and why there are no links

Sources are named by author and year with a one-line description of what was actually found, and no URLs are given. Three reasons: links rot, a link often points at a press summary rather than the paper, and a tidy link list creates an impression of verification this pack has not performed. Anyone quoting these to a user should look up the primary source first.

Do not add fabricated citations to this file or to any coaching output. A wrong citation is worse than no citation, because it is checkable and it destroys the credibility of everything around it. If a claim cannot be attributed accurately, label it **[E3]** and present it as craft.

## 3. The replication problem in this specific literature

Social psychology's replication crisis hit exactly the subfields this OS touches. The Open Science Collaboration's large replication project (2015) reproduced a minority of the effects it attempted, with substantially smaller effect sizes on average. Several of the most quotable results in the attraction and body-language literature are among the casualties.

Consequences the OS enforces:

1. Pre-2011 single-study findings with a memorable narrative are treated as **[E2]** at best until a replication is known.
2. Any claim widely repeated in popular dating material is presumed contaminated until checked.
3. The absence of a strong evidence base is stated rather than papered over, because the alternative is borrowing authority the field does not have.

This is not nihilism about the research. Some of it is solid and is used below. It is a filter.

## 4. What is reasonably well established (E1)

Used with confidence, still with the individual-variance caveat.

**Proximity and repeated exposure.** Physical and functional proximity strongly predicts who people form relationships with (Festinger, Schachter and Back, 1950, on housing-unit friendships). Repeated exposure to a neutral stimulus increases liking (Zajonc, 1968; Bornstein's 1989 meta-analysis), and it holds for people in a shared setting (Moreland and Beach, 1992, on attendance in a classroom). This is the strongest practical argument in the whole pack, and it is why [dating-strategy.md](dating-strategy.md) ranks recurring shared activity above every other context.

**Reciprocity of liking.** Learning that someone likes you is among the most consistent predictors of liking them back, across many studies and paradigms. It is the empirical case for legibility of interest ([ethics-and-consent.md](ethics-and-consent.md) section 9) and against strategic ambiguity.

**Reciprocal self-disclosure builds closeness.** Escalating, matched, mutual disclosure produces closeness in a short time (Aron and colleagues, 1997, the closeness-generating procedure often called the 36 questions), and disclosure and liking reinforce each other (Collins and Miller's 1994 meta-analysis). The reciprocity matters: matched disclosure outperforms one-sided disclosure (Sprecher and colleagues, 2013). This is the engine described in [conversation-craft.md](conversation-craft.md).

**Similarity in values and attitudes predicts attraction**, with the important qualification that perceived similarity does most of the work and actual similarity does less (Montoya, Horton and Kirchner, 2008).

**Warmth and competence are the two dimensions people read fastest** in social perception (Fiske, Cuddy and Glick, 2007). Optimizing one and neglecting the other is a legible failure mode, and it is why the core equation in [SKILL.md](../SKILL.md) carries both.

**Stated partner preferences predict actual attraction poorly.** People's explicit lists of what they want do not predict who they are drawn to when they meet them (Eastwick and Finkel, 2008; Eastwick, Luchies, Finkel and Hunt, 2014, meta-analysis). Use this with users who have a rigid checklist, and with users who believe they know exactly why they are not chosen.

**Attractiveness matters less as acquaintance lengthens.** Assortative matching on physical attractiveness weakens the longer two people have known each other before dating (Hunt, Eastwick and Finkel, 2015). This is the empirical backing for the slow-path argument in [attraction-fundamentals.md](attraction-fundamentals.md).

**Physical attractiveness has real but moderate effects**, not the decisive ones the culture assumes (Langlois and colleagues, 2000, meta-analysis of the attractiveness literature).

**Avoidance maintains anxiety, and graded exposure reduces it.** The clinical literature on exposure is strong, and the modern framing emphasizes expectancy violation and inhibitory learning rather than habituation alone (Craske and colleagues, 2014). This underwrites [anxiety-and-avoidance.md](anxiety-and-avoidance.md), with the clinical boundary in [safety-and-boundaries.md](safety-and-boundaries.md).

**Safety behaviours and post-event processing maintain social anxiety** (Clark and Wells, 1995, and the large literature after it). This is why the ladder forbids the drink, the phone and the rehearsed line.

**Social rejection engages neural systems overlapping with physical pain processing** (Eisenberger, Lieberman and Williams, 2003; Kross and colleagues, 2011). Interpretation of the overlap is debated, but the practical point stands: the hit is real, it is not weakness, and it passes.

**Variable-ratio reinforcement produces persistent, anxious, hard-to-extinguish behaviour.** This is basic and robust, and it is precisely why intermittent warmth is refused in [refusals.md](refusals.md). The mechanism works. That is the problem with it.

## 5. What is promising but thin (E2)

**Arousal misattribution.** The bridge study (Dutton and Aron, 1974) is famous, small, and open to alternative explanations. Treat "take them somewhere exciting" as weakly supported at best.

**Self-compassion.** Associated with resilience under failure, with intervention evidence that is real but modest and often self-report based (Neff and colleagues). Used in [rejection-resilience.md](rejection-resilience.md) as an E2 practice.

**Attachment styles.** Useful as a self-understanding frame, dimensional rather than categorical, moderately stable. Not a diagnostic instrument, and specifically not a tool for classifying someone else ([refusals.md](refusals.md) Tier 3).

**Responsive versus spontaneous desire.** The circular model of desire (Basson, 2000, and later work) reframes "I do not feel like it until we start" as a normal pattern rather than a deficit. Clinically influential, evidence base moderate. Used in [long-term-desire.md](long-term-desire.md).

**Relationship maintenance findings.** Positive-to-negative interaction ratios, turning toward bids, and repair after conflict come largely from observational work with real predictive claims that have drawn methodological criticism (the Gottman program, and critiques of post-hoc model fitting). Describe the direction, never quote a precise ratio as a law.

**Sexual overperception.** The tendency for some people to over-read sexual interest (Haselton and Buss, 2000, error management theory) is a real finding with contested explanations and mixed replications. The OS uses it only in the direction that reduces harm: assume you may be over-reading, and check ([social-calibration.md](social-calibration.md)).

**More information can reduce liking.** Learning more about a person can lower attraction rather than raise it (Norton, Frost and Ariely, 2007). Relevant to over-long profiles and to months of texting before meeting ([digital-and-apps.md](digital-and-apps.md)).

**Online dating as a research object.** The comprehensive critical review (Finkel, Eastwick, Karney, Reis and Sprecher, 2012) found no support for matching-algorithm claims and identified real costs to browsing many profiles. The shift of meeting online has since become dominant (Rosenfeld, Thomas and Hausen, 2019). Both are used in [digital-and-apps.md](digital-and-apps.md).

## 6. What is craft knowledge (E3)

The largest category in this pack, and the OS says so rather than hiding it. Craft knowledge here means: coherent, widely converged upon by skilled practitioners and by ordinary socially fluent people, mechanistically plausible, and not experimentally established for this application.

Everything in this list is labelled **[E3]** wherever it appears:

- the asymmetry read (who is doing the work in a conversation is the most informative single signal);
- the soft no as a protective format, and the practice of decoding it fast;
- the clean exit as the most important rep;
- one true thing about yourself as a conversational unit;
- statements as invitations outperforming a chain of questions;
- ending a conversation while it is still good;
- the meeting-in-the-middle principle for physical proximity;
- short, low-stakes, walkable first dates;
- the fit, condition, context ordering in self-presentation;
- pace-matching in messaging;
- the uniform strategy;
- the practice of writing a prediction before an interaction and scoring the read afterwards.

Craft is not a lesser category. It is most of what skilled people actually know. It is only dangerous when it is dressed as science.

## 7. What is taste (P)

Not empirically answerable, and the OS refuses to pretend otherwise: clothing style, humour register, hair, facial hair, tattoos, scent, music, how forward to be, how quickly to move, what a good date is, what is charming versus too much, who pays, and every question of the form "do people find X attractive". Individual and cultural variance dominates, and any universal prescription is a signal that the source is selling something.

The correct move on a **[P]** question is to give the tradeoffs, name the contexts where each option reads differently, and hand the decision back.

## 8. Claims that failed, and are still repeated

Users will bring these. Correct them briefly and without contempt.

| Claim | Status |
| --- | --- |
| "Power poses raise testosterone and lower cortisol" | The hormonal and behavioural effects failed to replicate (Ranehill and colleagues, 2015), and the original first author publicly stopped endorsing the effect (Carney, 2016). Some self-reported feelings of power survive. Stand comfortably because it makes you easier to be near, not because of a hormone story. **[E2]** at most. |
| "93 percent of communication is nonverbal" | A misapplication of Mehrabian's work on judging incongruent single-word emotional messages. The number does not describe communication in general. Nonverbal signalling matters, that statistic does not exist. |
| "Willpower is a depleting resource" | Ego depletion failed large multi-lab replication (Hagger and colleagues, 2016). Do not build a practice plan on it. |
| "Priming words changes behaviour reliably" | The famous social priming demonstrations largely failed to replicate (for example Doyen and colleagues, 2012). |
| "Men and women are hardwired to want opposite things" | Stated preferences differ by group on surveys and predict actual attraction poorly (Eastwick and Finkel). The classic casual-sex-offer study (Clark and Hatfield, 1989) is heavily confounded, and the gap narrows substantially when perceived safety and proposer quality are controlled (Conley, 2011). Treat sweeping biological claims as **[P]** and culturally contingent. |
| "Alpha and beta body language" | Not a taxonomy of human beings. Whatever is true underneath it (relaxed posture and appropriate use of space read well) is in [presence-and-embodiment.md](presence-and-embodiment.md) without the mythology. |
| "It takes 21 days to form a habit" | Not from a study of habit formation. Formation times vary widely. |
| "You have 7 seconds to make an impression" | People do form trait impressions from faces very fast (Willis and Todorov, 2006), but that research is about the speed of judgments, not their accuracy, and impressions update with interaction. |
| "Attachment style tells you how to handle them" | Attachment is a self-understanding frame **[E2]**, not an instrument to apply to another person. |

## 9. Where the research is systematically unrepresentative

Stated plainly, because it changes how much any of this transfers:

- Samples are dominated by young, Western, educated, mostly heterosexual, mostly university-student participants. Speed-dating studies in particular sample a narrow slice.
- Same-sex attraction, non-monogamous structures, disabled people, older adults, and most of the world's cultures are thinly represented across the attraction literature.
- Neurodivergent social signalling is largely absent, and much of the "read the signals" literature implicitly assumes a shared signalling convention that does not hold across neurotypes.
- Almost everything is short-term and laboratory-bound. Long relationship trajectories are studied with different, mostly observational, methods.

Practical consequence: when coaching outside the sampled slice, weight craft, ask the user about their own context, and drop the confident tone. Say "I do not know how this transfers to your situation" when that is the truth.

## 10. The clinical layer (C)

Where a pattern crosses into clinical territory, the evidence question stops being interesting and the routing question takes over. Effective treatments exist for social anxiety disorder, depression, body dysmorphic disorder, obsessive patterns and trauma, and they outperform anything in this pack for those conditions. The individual-CBT evidence for social anxiety in particular is strong (for example Mayo-Wilson and colleagues, 2014, network meta-analysis).

The OS does not diagnose, does not treat, and does not run drills over a clinical presentation. See [safety-and-boundaries.md](safety-and-boundaries.md).

## 12. How to answer a user who cites a study

1. Ask what they think it implies for what they should do. The implication is usually wrong even when the study is real.
2. Check the claim against sections 4, 5 and 8 before agreeing with it.
3. If it is in section 8, correct it in one or two sentences and move on. Do not perform the correction.
4. If it is real, restate the effect at its actual size, in the population it was measured in, and say what it does not tell them.
5. Return to the thing that is actually blocking them, which is almost never the thing the study was about.
